**Saladin** is the sultan of Babylon, a character in two tales of the [[Book/decameron]]: the third of the First Day ([[ShortStory/melchisedech-three-rings]]) and the ninth of the Tenth Day ([[ShortStory/saladin-and-messer-torello]]). In the latter he is presented as a "most valiant lord" at the time when the Christians make the general passage to recover the Holy Land, and he is the figure around which the tale builds its own demonstration of [[DefinedTerm/magnificence]]: the narrator announces indeed that he wishes to show, with a long but pleasing story, "one of the magnificences of Saladin".

The trait common to the two tales is his largeness in requiting whoever has rendered him a service: in the Tenth Day he requites the courtesy of the knight who had lodged him; in the First Day he gives the Jew who lent him money "very great gifts" and holds him "ever for his friend". In the tale of the Tenth Day the text calls him besides "most shrewd", and there he acts first as a lord who dissimulates his own condition, then as a sovereign who exercises it without measure to the advantage of a private man.

## In the tale of the Tenth Day

Having learned beforehand that the passage is being prepared, Saladin decides to see in person the preparations of the Christian lords in order to provide himself the better. His affairs in Egypt being ordered, and feigning to go on pilgrimage, he crosses many Christian provinces in the guise of a merchant, accompanied by two of his greatest and wisest men and by three servants only, declaring to whoever questions him that he is a Cypriot merchant bound for Paris. Riding through Lombardy he is lodged at Pavia by messer Torello di Strà; the text signals his shrewdness in understanding at once that the knight had led him to his own house "by contrivance", so as to leave him no way of refusing the invitation. On taking leave, to Torello's declaration that he does not believe them to be merchants, he answers that it may yet come about that he show him "somewhat of our merchandise". Having traversed all the West, he puts back to sea and returns to Alexandria, fully informed, and disposes himself to the defence.

During the passage almost all the Christians who escaped the mortality of the camp are taken — "whatever it was, the art or the fortune of Saladin" — and imprisoned in several cities. Among them Torello, led to Alexandria, who in order not to make himself known takes to training birds and thereby comes to the notice of the sultan, who draws him out of prison and keeps him as his own falconer, calling him only "the Christian", without recognising him. The recognition comes about through a gesture: Torello smiles and makes with his mouth a motion that Saladin had noticed in his house at Pavia. Certain now of who is before him, the sultan says within himself: "God has given me the time to show this man how welcome his courtesy was to me!", leads him among his own garments so that he may recognise the robes given him by Torello's wife, and reveals himself by embracing him. From that moment he has him clothed in royal garments, presents him to the greatest barons with much praise of his worth and commands that he be honoured as much as his own person; and he says to him: "since God has sent you to me here, think that henceforth not I, but you are the lord here".

## The leave-taking and the gifts

When Torello, believing his wife remarried, lies down resolved to die, Saladin — who "loved him supremely" — seeks the cause of it, reproves him for not having told him sooner and promises to have him at Pavia within the term. He charges one of his own necromancers, of whose art he had already had experience, with finding the way to transport him in a single night upon a bed. In the leave-taking he declares that it would have been most dear to him to live with him, lords alike, in the government of his realm, and that he would have wished to send him home with the honour and the company his worth deserves; he begs him finally to return at least once to see him again and not to think it a burden to visit him with letters, promising to do for him more willingly than for any other man alive. Of Torello's wife, whom he had known at Pavia, he says that among all the ladies he ever thought to see, she is the one whose manners, ways and bearing — "let alone the beauty, which is a fading flower" — seem to him most to be commended.

The gifts with which he accompanies the return are listed minutely by the text: a most rich bed of velvet mattresses and cloths of gold with a coverlet worked in roundels of the largest pearls and precious stones, afterwards esteemed in the West an infinite treasure; a great crown, marked in such a way that it might be understood to be sent to Torello's lady; a ring with a carbuncle so shining that it seemed a lighted torch; a sword whose mounting was of great price; a clasp of pearls the like of which had never been seen; two very great basins of gold full of doubloons, and nets of pearls, rings and girdles. Before the departure he has Torello clothed in the Saracen manner and has one of his own very long bands wound about his head; then he kisses him and commends him to God.

Returned to Pavia, Torello signifies by several messengers his happy repatriation to Saladin, holding himself his friend and his servant.

## In the tale of the First Day

In the third tale of the First Day Saladin is characterised at the opening by a praise of his worth: this, says the text, "not only made him from a small man sultan of Babylon, but also made him have many victories over the Saracen and Christian kings". The tale takes him, however, at a moment of difficulty: having spent all his treasure in divers wars and in very great magnificences of his own, and finding himself, on account of an accident that had supervened, in need of a good quantity of money without seeing whence to draw it so promptly, there comes into his mind the rich Jew Melchisedech, who lent at usury in Alexandria.

The trait the tale sets in relief is the renunciation of open violence. Saladin judges the Jew so avaricious that he would never lend of his own will, but "he did not wish to do him violence": pressed by need, he resolves rather to do him "a violence coloured by some reason", that is to construct a juridical pretext. He has him called, receives him familiarly, has him sit beside him and asks him which of the three laws — the Jewish, the Saracen or the Christian — he reputes the true one, reckoning to catch him in his words in order to be able to raise a question against him.

Defeated by the parable of the three rings, Saladin recognises that his interlocutor has excellently known how to come out of the snare laid before his feet, and reacts without resentment: he frankly opens his need to him and confesses to him also what he had had in mind to do had he not answered him with so much discretion. Having freely obtained the loan, he afterwards satisfies it entirely, gives him very great gifts, holds him ever for a friend and maintains him about his person in great and honourable estate — an outcome that anticipates, on a lesser scale, the largeness in requiting that the tale of the Tenth Day will develop in full.
