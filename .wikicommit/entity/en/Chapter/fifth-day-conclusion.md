The *Conclusion* of the Fifth Day is the section with which the fifth day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. In it is fixed the theme of the Sixth Day — that of witticisms and ready retorts — and in it is found the only place in the frame where a member of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] is called to order with an explicit threat on the part of whoever reigns.

The tale of [[Person/dioneo]] being ended, and the women having laughed at it "less for shame [...] than for little delight", the queen [[Person/fiammetta]] recognises that the end of her rule has come.

## Contents

Fiammetta rises to her feet, draws off the crown of laurel and pleasantly sets it on the head of [[Person/elissa]], saying to her: "To you, madam, it now falls to command". Elissa, having received the honour, does as had been done before: she first gives order with the steward for what is needful for the time of her lordship, then she enunciates the theme. She observes that it has already been heard many times how "with fine witticisms or with ready retorts or with quick presence of mind" many have known how "with a due bite to blunt others' teeth or to drive away oncoming perils"; and since the matter is fair and may be useful, she wishes that on the morrow they discourse within these bounds, that is, "of those who, being assailed, extricated themselves with some graceful witticism, or with a ready retort or presence of mind fled loss or peril or scorn" (see [[DefinedTerm/ready-retort]]). The proposal is much commended by all, and the queen dismisses the company until the hour of supper.

The cicadas having by now ceased their singing, all are called back to supper; this being ended "with glad festivity", they give themselves to singing and to playing. [[Person/emilia]] having led a dance at the new queen's wish, Dioneo is commanded to sing a song, and he promptly begins "Mistress Aldruda, lift up your tail — for I bring you good tidings". All the women begin to laugh, and most of all the queen, who commands him to leave that one and say another.

Dioneo retorts by proposing a series of them — he says he would know "more than a thousand" — and at each refusal of the queen's he offers another of the same kind, alleging that he has not the timbrel for some of them. Elissa, "a little troubled", while all the others laugh, warns him: "Dioneo, leave off jesting and give us a fine one: and if not, thou mightest find out how I know how to grow angry". Dioneo, hearing this, leaves off his prattle and promptly begins the ballata "Love, the charming light" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), which sings the light kindled by the beloved's eyes and prays Love to make her feel something of his fire.

## Framing and Significance

Dioneo having shown by his silence the end of his own song, the queen has several others said, having nonetheless much commended his. A good part of the night being past, and the heat of the day being felt already vanquished by the coolness of the night, she commands that each go to rest until the following day.

The episode is significant for the way in which the frame regulates itself: the [[DefinedTerm/dioneos-privilege]] exempts Dioneo from the theme of the tales, not from the proprieties of song, and the sovereign here exercises her authority upon a ground that the privilege does not cover. The theme that Elissa fixes for the following day, centred on the ready word that defends whoever utters it, serves as an immediate counterpoint to a scene in which it is precisely verbal wit that is restrained.
