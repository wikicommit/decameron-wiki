The *frame narrative* is the structure within which the tales of the [[Book/decameron]] are gathered: it is made up of everything that does not belong to the development of the tales, that is, of the Florence contaminated by the plague and of the story of the group of young people who decide to withdraw to the countryside. Several narrative levels intersect within it: the plane of the author, who often intervenes in the first person; that of the life of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]]; that of the tales proper; and that of the reflections on the stories narrated, elaborated by the storytellers as a preamble to, or in the wake of, the individual tales.

## Origin and Diffusion

The use of a narrative frame in which to insert tales is of Indian origin; this structure then passed into Arabic literature and into the West, and was typical of the Eastern and Arabic novella tradition on which Boccaccio drew. Another precedent is constituted by the *Saturnalia* of Macrobius, in which the idea of a company of ten people conversing after dinner over several days already appears, while in the *Metamorphoses* of Apuleius is found the theme of telling tales in a situation of danger, in the face of death.

## Use in the Decameron

The frame of the *Decameron* arises from the [[Event/black-death-1348]]: seven young women and three young men of elevated social condition meet in the church of [[Place/santa-maria-novella]] and take refuge on the hills near [[Place/florence]], where they entertain themselves by telling each other tales in turn. It is for this reason that Boccaccio at the beginning of the work gives a long and detailed description of the disease that struck Florence in 1348. Each day is ruled by a king or a queen who sets its theme, with the exception of the first and the ninth, whose subject is free.

The young people create a sort of almost perfect parallel reality, in order to demonstrate how man, thanks to his own powers and his own intelligence, is capable of giving an order to things — a theme that would later be fundamental to Humanism. Set against the uniform world of the brigata are the tales, which have an autonomous life and describe above all the mercantile and bourgeois reality. It is precisely the frame that makes the *Decameron* a unique work and not a mere collection of tales: the latter are all connected to one another through it, forming a sort of novel. According to Maurizio Fiorilla, the formation of the brigata described in the second part of the *Introduction* to the First Day "creates the conditions for a reconstruction of the world through literature".

## The Institution of the Frame in the Introduction

The moment at which the frame is instituted is the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]], where its mechanisms are enunciated by the characters themselves. It is [[Person/pampinea]] who, once the company has reached the place in the country, argues that "things that are without order cannot long endure" and proposes that there be among them a chief to be honoured and obeyed as their superior, and that the burden and the honour of the lordship be assigned to each for one day, so that no one who has not experienced them may envy them: the first elected by all, the following ones designated, as the hour of vespers draws near, by whoever has ruled the day, with the power to order and dispose of the time, the place and the manner of life of the company.

Elected the first queen, Pampinea is crowned with a laurel garland woven by [[Person/filomena]], which for the whole duration of the company will be "a manifest sign to every other of the royal lordship and pre-eminence"; she further commands that no one bring in from outside "any tidings other than glad ones", thereby sanctioning the separation from the plague-stricken city on the plane of discourse as well. The choice of storytelling is in turn given a motive: in the afternoon the queen proposes to pass the hot part of the day not with the backgammon boards and chessboards available there, because in a game the mind of one of the parties must needs be troubled without much pleasure to the other or to the onlookers, but rather by telling tales, since the narrating of one person can afford delight to the whole company that listens. Having established a free theme for the first day, she turns to [[Person/panfilo]], seated on her right, that he may make a beginning.

## The Mechanism of Succession

The rule that gives the frame its recurring rhythm is formulated in the Conclusion of the First Day, when [[Person/pampinea]], at the end of her own reign, does not merely designate her successor but also fixes the hour at which the operation is to be carried out. Since "whoever does not take some time beforehand does not seem able to provide well for the future", and so that whatever the new queen decides for the following morning may be prepared, she judges that at that hour — the sun already declining toward vespers — the following days should begin.

In the same section the other two mechanisms that the frame will repeat nine times over are instituted. The first is the theme announced in advance: [[Person/filomena]], having received the garland, begins "to confine within some bounds that of which we are to tell tales, and to set it before you beforehand", so that each may have space to think of a fine tale on the given proposal. The second is the dispensation from that theme, [[DefinedTerm/dioneos-privilege]], granted at the very moment when the rule comes into being.

The frame closes at last with the gesture symmetrical to the initial one: in the Conclusion of the Tenth Day the king keeps the crown until their departure instead of passing it on, thus breaking the chain of successions.
