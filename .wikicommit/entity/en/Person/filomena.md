Filomena is one of the seven young women of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]]. Her name has been interpreted as "lover of song" or as "she who is loved"; it is to her that [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] dedicates the [[Book/filostrato]].

Together with [[Person/pampinea]] and [[Person/elissa]], Filomena stands out within the work for her own sense of initiative.

## Role in the brigata

In the introduction Filomena's words serve to correct Pampinea's opening speech: she is aware that a woman needs the presence of a man at her side, inasmuch as women are creatures too frail. To her is owed, moreover, the choice of a fixed theme for each day, which serves as a connecting thread for all the tales and constitutes one of the pivots of the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] of the work.

## Her tales

Among the tales told by Filomena, those stand out most in which the life of the woman is determined by the choices of the man; one of the most famous is the tale of [[ShortStory/lisabetta-da-messina]].

The text of the philological edition confirms this attribution: the tale of [[Person/elissa]] having ended and been somewhat commended by the king, it is Filomena who is bidden to discourse, and she takes the word "all full of compassion for the wretched Gerbino and his lady", after a piteous sigh. In prefacing her account she warns that it will not be "of folk of so high a condition" as those of whom it has just been narrated, "but it will perchance be no less piteous", and declares that she was drawn to it by the mention of Messina made shortly before.

## Her reign

Filomena is the second to govern the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]]: at the end of the First Day [[Person/pampinea]] places the laurel garland upon her head. "Somewhat reddened for shame", she first of all reconfirms the offices already given by her predecessor and disposes what is to be done for the following morning and supper; then she declares that she does not wish to follow her own judgement alone "but yours together with mine".

It is here that the choice of the fixed theme, already attributed to her by the critical tradition, is accomplished in the text: what Pampinea could not do "for being elected late to the governance", Filomena begins, that is "to restrain within some term that whereof we are to tell tales, and to show it to you beforehand", so that each may have room to think of a fair tale upon the proposal given. The theme she fixes for the Second Day is "of those who, harassed by divers things, have come beyond their hope to a happy end". Immediately afterwards it is of her that [[Person/dioneo]] asks the [[DefinedTerm/dioneos-privilege]]: knowing him for "a merry and festive man" and perceiving that he asks it in order to be able to gladden the weary company, Filomena grants it to him gladly with the consent of the others.

In the Introduction to the Second Day the text describes her as "shapely and of very pleasing aspect", crowned with her laurel garland: having looked upon the whole company in the face, she commands [[Person/neifile]] to give beginning to the tales.
