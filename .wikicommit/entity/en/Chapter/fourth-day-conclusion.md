The *Conclusion* of the Fourth Day is the section with which the fourth day of the [[Book/decameron]], devoted to the loves that had an unhappy end, closes. It is the only section in which the outgoing sovereign excuses himself to the company for the matter he has imposed, and in which the theme of the following day is avowedly constructed as a remedy for the one just concluded.

If the first tales had saddened "the breasts of the charming ladies", the last one, by [[Person/dioneo]], makes them laugh so much — "and especially when he said that the judge had the hook fastened on" — that they can restore themselves from the compassion they had felt for the others.

## Contents

Seeing that the sun begins "to turn yellow" and that the term of his lordship has come, [[Person/filostrato]] excuses himself in very pleasant words for what he has done, "that is, for having made them discourse upon matter as harsh as that of the unhappiness of lovers". The excuse made, he rises to his feet, takes the laurel from his head and sets it "upon the most golden head of [[Person/fiammetta]]", saying that he lays that crown upon her as upon one who will know better than any other how to reconsole her companions, with tomorrow's day, for the harsh day just past.

The text lingers over the portrait of the new queen: the hair "curling, long and of gold" that falls over her white and delicate shoulders, the roundish face "all resplendent with a true colour of white lilies and red roses mingled", two eyes "that seemed those of a peregrine falcon" and "a little small mouth whose lips seemed two little rubies". Fiammetta accepts smiling and, "so that thou mayest the better perceive what thou hast done", commands from that very moment that each prepare to discourse on the morrow "of what befell happily to some lover after certain harsh or unfortunate mishaps". The proposition pleases everyone; the queen sends for the steward, disposes with him the things needful, and gladly dismisses the brigata until the hour of supper.

## Framing and Significance

The company scatters, part into the garden "whose beauty was not to be tired of too soon", part towards the mills that ground outside it, taking "divers delights according to their divers appetites" until supper. This is taken beside the fair fountain, as is customary; then they give themselves to dancing and singing, and while [[Person/filomena]] leads the dance the queen turns to Filostrato. Since she is certain "that thy songs are such as thy tales are", and so that more days may not be troubled by his misfortunes, she wishes him to be the one to sing one, whichever best pleases him.

Filostrato then sings the ballata "Weeping I show" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), the lament of one who grieves "at having been betrayed by Love under trust" and asks for death as his only comfort. The words of the song show "clearly enough what Filostrato's mind was, and the cause of it"; and the text adds that the countenance of a woman who was in the dance would perhaps have declared it still better, had the darkness of the night that had come on not hidden her blush. That one being ended, many others are sung until the hour of going to sleep, when, at the queen's command, each withdraws to their own chamber.

The section shows the frame correcting itself: the theme of each day is born not only from the inclination of whoever reigns, but also in reaction to the effect produced on the brigata by the previous day.
