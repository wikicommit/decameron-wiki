Naples is the city in which [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] lived from 1327 to 1340, where his father acted as exchange agent for the family of the Bardi. Boccaccio arrived there at fourteen and described it as "... a most ancient city and perhaps as delightful as, or more than, any other in Italy", finding there a reality wholly different from that of [[Place/florence]]: if Florence was a strongly provincial communal city, Naples was instead the seat of a royal and cosmopolitan court, that of the Angevins.

Boccaccio's bond with Naples was intense and fruitful. He lived there for several years "... among noble youths", frequented the rich royal library and the intellectuals of the court, grew passionate about literary studies, discovered his love for writing and met [[Person/fiammetta]], his inspiring muse. Even after his return to Florence in 1340 he sought all his life to return there.

## Geography and places

In the basilica of San Lorenzo Maggiore there took place, on 30 March 1336, Boccaccio's first meeting with the woman who was to become his inspiring muse. At the University of Naples his father enrolled him in law; according to Natalino Sapegno he there followed for one academic year (1330-31) the lessons of the poet and jurist Cino da Pistoia, preferring however to draw near to the poetic lessons which the man of Pistoia imparted outside the academic environment.

## History and significance

King Robert of Anjou (1277-1343) was an extremely cultivated and pious sovereign, so passionate for culture as to possess a notable library, managed by the erudite Paolo da Perugia. At the Angevin court Boccaccio came to know, besides Paolo da Perugia, also Andalò del Negro, and probably learned some grammatical and lexical notions of Greek from the Byzantine monk and theologian Barlaam of Seminara. In the cosmopolitan Neapolitan climate — where the French chivalric ethic imported by the Angevins, Arab-Byzantine influences, courtly erudition and the presence of cultivators of the Dantean memory all met — Boccaccio gave scope to the experimentalism that characterises works such as the [[Book/filocolo]], the [[Book/filostrato]] and the [[Book/teseida]].

His Neapolitan youth was not, however, exhausted in the frequenting of academic and courtly circles alone: the tales and the adventures of merchants which Boccaccio heard while he served at the commercial bank were to be fundamental for the great narrative fresco that would come to life with the [[Book/decameron]].
