The *Proem* is the section with which the [[Book/decameron]] of [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] opens, placed before the First Day. The opening rubric announces the book in terms that fix at once its title and its structure: "here begins the book called decameron, in which are contained a hundred tales told in ten days by seven women and by three young men" (the source gives the rubric entirely in lower case).

The text opens with the sentence "It is a human thing to have compassion for the afflicted" and proceeds on an avowedly autobiographical plane: the author recounts having been inflamed, from earliest youth until that moment, with a love "most high and noble", perhaps higher than his "low condition" would have required. That love, he specifies, was of the greatest travail to him not through any cruelty of the woman loved, but through the excess of fire conceived in his mind by an ill-governed appetite; what relieved him were the pleasant discourses and praiseworthy consolations of a friend, so much so that he declares himself firmly convinced that it is precisely for this that he did not die. In time the love abated of itself, since — so Boccaccio — it pleased Him who is infinite to give as an immutable law that all worldly things should have an end.

From this descends the declared motive of the work: since gratitude is among the virtues most highly to be praised and its contrary to be blamed, the author proposes, so as not to seem ungrateful, to render in exchange for what he received some "alleviation" — if not to those who helped him, who perhaps have no need of it, at least to those who do. Comfort is to be offered where the need appears greater, and on this criterion rests the choice of the recipient.

## Contents

The Proem contains no tales and is articulated in three successive movements. The first is the autobiographical account of the youthful love and of the comfort received from friends, presented as a debt of gratitude to be discharged. The second is the argument concerning the recipient: the gift befits "charming ladies" far more than men, because they keep the flames of love hidden within their delicate breasts and, constrained by the wills, the pleasures and the commandments of fathers, mothers, brothers and husbands, dwell for the greater part of their time "within the small compass of their chambers", where melancholy lingers with grievous tedium if it is not removed by new discourse; men in love, by contrast, have many ways of lightening the weight of their thoughts — going about, hearing and seeing many things, fowling, hunting, fishing, riding, gaming and trading.

The third movement is the announcement of the content and of the end. To amend in part the "sin of fortune", which was more sparing of support precisely where the strength was less, the author declares that he means to tell "a hundred tales, or fables or parables or histories, whichever we wish to call them", narrated over ten days by an "honourable brigata of seven women and three young men" in the "pestilential time of the late mortality", together with some little songs sung by those same women for their delight. In them will be seen "pleasant and harsh cases of love and other fortuitous happenings", occurring in modern times as well as in ancient ones. The text closes with thanks to Love, who by freeing the author from his own bonds has granted him leave to attend to the pleasure of his women readers.

## Framing and Significance

The Proem institutes the author's voice as a narrative instance distinct from that of the storytellers: it is Boccaccio in the first person who declares the intention of telling, while the telling proper will be entrusted to the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]]. There are already enunciated in it, in compendious form, the load-bearing elements of the work's [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]]: the number of the tales, the duration of ten days, the composition of the group of narrators and the circumstance of the pestilence.

No less explicit is the definition of the audience and of the purpose. The work is addressed "in succour and refuge of those women who love", while for other women, the author observes, "the needle, the spindle and the reel" suffice; from the reading the recipients will be able to draw at once delight for the diverting things displayed and useful counsel, recognising "that which is to be fled and that which is likewise to be followed". This double finality of pleasure and of instruction, declared before the book has even begun, is the criterion the author hands to the reader for interpreting the hundred tales that follow.
