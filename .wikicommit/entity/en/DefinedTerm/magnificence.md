**Being magnificent** is the quality that several narrators of the Tenth Day of the [[Book/decameron]] declare they mean to tell of. In the two tales documented here the notion receives two complementary treatments: in the first tale a definition by simile, which places it above the other virtues; in the eighth, an argument about the relation between the magnificent deed and the condition of the one who performs it.

## Definition

Opening the first tale of the day, the narrator to whom the king has assigned the task of "telling of magnificence" gives a definition by image: it, "as the sun is the beauty and ornament of all the heavens, is the brightness and light of every other virtue". Magnificence is therefore presented not as one virtue alongside the others, but as that which makes them bright and luminous; and the narrator declares herself bound in the greatest gratitude for having been set over so great a matter.

The tale she tells immediately afterwards turns upon a king — Alfonso of Spain — who, having shown the knight messer Ruggeri de' Figiovanni by the trial of the two chests that fortune and not he is to blame for the reward withheld, gives him "in despite" of fortune the chest that fortune had taken from him (see [[ShortStory/ruggeri-de-figiovanni]]).

## Who Is Held to It

In the eighth tale of the same day [[Person/filomena]] proceeds from the same quality to justify a different choice of matter. It belongs first of all to the powerful: "who does not know that kings can, when they will, do every great thing, and that being magnificent is most especially required of them?" But for that very reason it is not measured by the greatness of the deed in itself: one who, having the power, does what belongs to him "does well, but a man ought not to marvel so greatly at it nor exalt it with the highest praise", whereas greater wonder and greater praise are deserved by another who performs the same act though held to it less, "one of whom, for want of power, less should be required".

Hence the choice of matter. If her listeners extol the deeds of kings with so many words and find them fair, much more — the narrator argues — must those of their own equals please and be commended, when they are equal to or greater than those of kings; and she therefore proposes to tell of "a praiseworthy and magnificent deed performed between two citizens who were friends". The qualification of *magnificent* thus falls upon a deed performed between private persons and not upon a deed of kings; what that act so qualified may be, the text does not say.

The relation to [[DefinedTerm/friendship]] is affirmed explicitly in the encomium with which the eighth tale closes, where friendship is called "the most discerning mother of magnificence and of honesty": in the text's formulation magnificence is not the presupposition of friendship, but that which is born of it.
