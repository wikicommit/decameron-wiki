The *Conclusion* of the Eighth Day is the section with which the eighth day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. In it is accomplished the only suspension of the obligatory theme in the entire work: [[Person/emilia]], crowned queen, decides not to confine the matter of the following day within any particular subject, letting each discourse "according as it pleases him".

## Contents

[[Person/dioneo]]'s tale being ended, [[Person/lauretta]] recognises that the term has come beyond which she must reign no longer; having commended the counsel of Pietro del Canigiano, "which appeared good by its effect", and Salabaetto's sagacity in carrying it into execution, she lifts the laurel from her head and sets it upon Emilia's, saying in ladylike fashion: "Madam, I do not know how pleasing a queen we shall have in you, but a fair one we shall assuredly have: see to it, then, that your deeds answer to your beauty."

Emilia blushes a little, not so much at being made queen as at seeing herself praised in public for that of which women are wont to be most desirous, and becomes in her face "as new roses are at dawn"; then, having settled with the seneschal the matters pertaining to the company, she takes the word.

Her reasoning proceeds by two similes. The first is that of the oxen: after they have laboured some part of the day "confined beneath the yoke", they are seen relieved and loosed from the yoke and let go freely to pasture through the woods, wherever it most pleases them. The second is that of gardens: "gardens leafy with various plants are seen to be not less fair, but far more so, than the woods in which we see only oaks". Having regard, then, to how many days they have discoursed "confined under a certain law", Emilia judges that to roam a while — and in roaming to regain strength for returning beneath the yoke — is "not only […] useful but opportune". She therefore does not intend to confine them within any particular subject, holding firmly that the variety of the things told will be no less pleasing than having spoken of one alone; and she observes that, having done thus, whoever succeeds her in the realm will be able with greater assurance to confine them within the accustomed laws, "as being stronger".

## Framing and Significance

Each commends the queen "as a wise woman"; having risen, the ladies set to making garlands and to diverting themselves, the young men to games and to singing, until the hour of supper, taken beside the fair fountain "with festivity and with pleasure". After supper, singing and dancing, the queen — "to follow the style of her predecessors" and although some had already sung of their own accord — commands [[Person/panfilo]] to sing one; and he freely begins the ballata "Tanto è, Amore, il bene" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), which declares an amorous happiness so high that it can neither be told nor shown, and which must indeed be concealed because, were it heard, "it would turn to torment".

To the song all answer fully, but no one fails to note its words "with more attentive solicitude than pertained to him", striving to guess what he was singing of having to keep hidden; and although various people went on imagining various things, "none for all that arrived at the truth of the matter". Then, seeing the song ended and the young ladies and the men glad to rest, the queen commands that each go off to sleep.

Emilia's decision is significant because it explicitly formulates the principle upon which the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] rests: the obligatory theme is an accepted yoke, whose usefulness depends on its being periodically loosened.
