The *Introduction* to the Third Day is the section that opens the third day of the [[Book/decameron]] and precedes the First Tale. In it is accomplished the removal decided upon by [[Person/neifile]] at the end of the Second Day, and in it is read the most extended description of a place in the whole frame: that of the walled garden of the second palace, which occupies by itself the greater part of the section.

## Contents

The dawn is beginning to turn orange when, on the Sunday, the queen rises and makes the whole company rise. The steward has been sent ahead "a good while before" to the place where they were to go, with the things needful and with those who should prepare there what was necessary; seeing the queen already on the way, he has everything else loaded and departs with the baggage and with the household that remained. The [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] proceeds at a slow pace "guided by the song of perhaps twenty nightingales and other birds", along a little-trodden path full of grasses and flowers, towards the west; and without having gone beyond two thousand paces, well before the middle of terce, it comes to "a most beautiful and rich palace", set somewhat raised above the plain upon a little hill.

The palace being gone through, the company praises the great halls and the chambers "clean and adorned", and deems its lord magnificent; having come down to the ground floor, it praises still more the wide and glad courtyard, the vaults full of excellent wines and the ice-cold water that springs there in great abundance. Upon a loggia that commands the courtyard, the "discreet steward" receives and refreshes them with "most precious comfits and excellent wines".

He then has opened the garden beside the palace, "which was all walled round about". The paths, most wide and "all straight as an arrow", are covered with pergolas of vines then in flower, and closed at the sides by rose bushes white and red and by jasmines, so that one may go there in the shade even with the sun high. In the midst is a meadow of grass most fine "and so green that it seemed almost black", painted with perhaps a thousand varieties of flowers and enclosed by orange and citron trees bearing together the old fruits, the new ones and the blossoms. At the centre of the meadow is a fountain of whitest marble with "marvellous carvings", from which a figure set upon a column throws towards the sky so much water and so high that, falling back with a delightful sound, "with less a mill would have ground"; the overflowing water goes out by a hidden way and by little channels "very fair and skilfully made" runs through the whole garden, then descends towards the plain and turns, "with the greatest force and with no small profit to the lord", two mills.

Seeing the garden, its fair order, the plants and the fountain with its rivulets, all begin to affirm that, "if paradise could be made on earth", they would not know how to give it any other form than that. Going about it and making themselves garlands of various boughs, they finally become aware of a beauty they had not yet noticed: the garden is full "of perhaps a hundred varieties of fair animals" — rabbits, hares, roe deer, young fawns and other kinds of harmless animals — that go about there to disport themselves "almost tame".

## Framing and Significance

The tables being set about the fountain, and six little songs having first been sung and some dances danced, the company goes to eat, served "in the greatest and fairest and most composed order"; then it returns to the airs, the songs and the dances until, the heat coming on, the queen judges it time that whoever wishes should go to sleep. Some go, others, "won by the beauty of the place", remain to read romances, to play at chess and at tables. Nones being past and their faces refreshed with the cool water, all gather in the meadow near the fountain and wait to tell tales upon the matter proposed by the queen; the first on whom she lays this charge is [[Person/filostrato]].

The section fixes the place in which the greater part of the following days will unfold, and establishes the fountain as the point about which the brigata eats, sits and tells its tales — a physical centre of the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] that the text will recall by formula on the days that follow.
