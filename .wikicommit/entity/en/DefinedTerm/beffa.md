In the *Decameron* the **beffa** is the trick that one character contrives at another's expense in order to make sport of him. The text of [[ShortStory/calandrino-and-the-heliotrope]] gives its most explicit formulation at the moment when the trickster conceives his design: having heard certain things about his victim's simplicity, he "purposed to take his pleasure of his doings by playing him some beffa or making him believe some strange thing". The two terms of the hendiadys indicate together the end — the trickster's amusement — and the means, namely the inducing of a false conviction in the victim; in the same tale the word then recurs to designate the completed enterprise, when one of the accomplices predicts that the target will remember "perhaps for a month this beffa".

## Usage

The beffa is distinguished from deception for gain because profit is not its motive: the one who contrives it seeks laughter, and what he takes from the victim is the correspondence between what she or he believes and what actually happens. Its precondition is therefore a declared disparity of shrewdness between the parties — in [[ShortStory/calandrino-and-the-heliotrope]] the tricksters are described as "very merry, but for the rest shrewd and sagacious", and they keep company with their target precisely because they take great delight in his manners and his simplicity.

The mechanism may be articulated across several hands: in the same tale a first trickster plants the false belief by feigning expertise in stones, and two others take it up and bring it to completion by behaving as though the believed effect were real. Its success requires no material contrivance, but only that the victim interpret as confirmations a series of signs that in themselves prove nothing. The beffa may moreover not close with a disclosure: it may leave the one tricked in his mistaken conviction, and even consolidate it by suggesting to him a false explanation of his own failure.

## The Beffa as the Theme of a Day

The beffa is the only subject to which the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] devotes two consecutive days, and in both cases the theme is formulated in the Conclusion of the preceding day.

At the end of the Sixth Day the king [[Person/dioneo]] establishes that the discourse shall be "of the tricks which, either for love or for their own deliverance, women have played upon their husbands, whether the husbands perceived them or not". The formulation is avowedly drawn from the words of the maidservant [[Person/licisca]], who that same morning had declared that she well knew "how many and what tricks" married women play upon their husbands; Dioneo sets aside the first part of what she had said, judging it "childish stuff", and retains the second. The two purposes that the definition admits — love and one's own deliverance — here restrict the beffa to a motivated act, distinct from mere amusement.

At the end of the Seventh Day the new queen [[Person/lauretta]] widens the definition. Observing with a witticism that, did she not fear to show herself "of the breed of the snappish cur, that will be avenged on the instant", she would by way of retaliation set the theme of tricks played by men upon their wives, she prefers to remove the restriction of gender altogether: the discourse shall be "of those tricks which every day either woman plays on man or man on woman or one man on another".

Discoursing on such matter provokes the only moral objection raised within the frame: to some of the ladies the theme imposed by Dioneo seems "ill to befit them", and they beg him to change it. The king defends it, observing that "the times are such" that, so long as men and women refrain from acting dishonourably, "every discourse is permitted" — the judges have left the tribunals, divine and human laws are silent — and that anyone who knew the company reluctant to speak of it "would perhaps suspect that you were guilty in the matter".
