**Bengodi** is the imaginary land of abundance described in the [[ShortStory/calandrino-and-the-heliotrope]]. It is not a place of the world of the tale but a place invented within it: it appears in the feigned lecture of a character who, pretending to be a "solemn and great lapidary", answers the question as to where the stones of virtue are to be found by saying that most of them are "in Berlinzone, land of the Basques, in a district that was called Bengodi".

## Geography and access

The description proceeds by images of food that produces itself and offers itself without labour. At Bengodi "they tie up the vines with sausages", a goose is to be had for a penny with a gosling thrown in, and there rises "a mountain all of grated parmesan cheese" upon whose summit stand folk who do nothing but make macaroni and ravioli, cook them in capon broth and cast them down the slope, "and he who took most of them had most". Hard by there runs "a little river of vernaccia, of the best that ever was drunk, without a drop of water in it". To the question who eats the capons used for the broth, the internal narrator answers that the Basques eat them all.

The distance too is given in a form that excludes all verification: asked how many miles off it lies, the trickster replies with a quibble void of sense, "There be more of them than a thousandty, that sings all night", and his interlocutor draws the conclusion, formulated with perfect gravity, that it must lie "further off than the Abruzzi". Asked whether he has ever been there, the other answers that he has been there "as much once as a thousand times".

## History and significance

In the structure of the tale Bengodi is not a decorative element but the testing of the deceit. It serves to verify how far the target is disposed to believe before the truly operative piece of news is proposed to him, that is the existence of the [[DefinedTerm/heliotrope-stone]] in the Mugnone: he who has accepted without objection the mountain of parmesan will accept also the stone that renders men invisible. The credulity of the character is moreover made explicit by the text, which notes how he gave to those words, spoken "with a steady face and without laughing", the faith that may be given to any most manifest truth, and how he answered by remarking that "that is a good country" and lamenting that it is too far off to go and see them "make those macaroni tumble down".

That the fiction has taken hold is confirmed further on in the tale: having taken his leave of the trickster, the protagonist reports to his companions also what he has heard of the district of Bengodi, "affirming with oaths that so it was".
