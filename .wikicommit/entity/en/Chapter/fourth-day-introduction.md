The *Introduction* to the Fourth Day is the section that opens the fourth day of the [[Book/decameron]]. It is distinguished from all the other introductions because the narrative of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] is suspended in it almost entirely: [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] speaks in it in the first person to the "dearest ladies", answers those who have criticised the tales already made public, and takes up the thread of the frame again only in the last paragraph.

The section is placed by the author himself at the midpoint of the undertaking: he observes that he has not yet "come to the third part of my labour" and fears that, his detractors receiving no repulse, they may multiply before he reaches the end.

## Contents

Boccaccio starts from the observation that he was mistaken: he supposed, from the words of the wise and from the things he had seen and read, that "the impetuous and burning wind of envy" would strike only the high towers or the loftiest crowns of the trees, and instead, though he has striven to flee it by going not only along the plains but "through the deepest valleys", he has been "fiercely shaken by it, indeed all but uprooted". Proof of it are the little tales, written in Florentine vernacular and in prose, "without a title" and "in a style as humble and lowly as they can possibly be".

The accusations are enumerated: that women please him too much and that it is not a seemly thing that he should take such delight in pleasing them and consoling them; that at his age "to go on running after these things" ill becomes him; that he would do more wisely to keep "with the Muses on Parnassus" than to mingle among women with "these prattlings"; that, more spitefully, he would do better to think "whence I am to get my bread"; and finally that the things he has told were otherwise than as he presents them.

Before answering, the author tells in his own favour the [[ShortStory/goslings-tale]] — avowedly "not an entire tale [...] but part of one" — and from it he sets his reply going. He confesses most openly that women please him and that he strives to please them, and asks whether one ought to marvel at it, given that even he who was reared upon a wild mountain, as soon as he saw them, desired them alone. To those who speak against his age he answers that "though the leek have a white head", it does not follow that "the tail is green", and recalls that Guido Cavalcanti and [[Person/dante-alighieri]] when already old, and messer Cino da Pistoia when "very old indeed", held it an honour to take pleasure in such things. As for the Muses, he observes that neither can men dwell always with them nor they with men, and that "the Muses are women": it was women who were the cause of his composing a thousand verses, whereas the Muses were never the cause of his making a single one — they did indeed help him, and showed him how to compose those thousand. To those who have compassion on his hunger he replies that poets have found more among their fables than many rich men among their treasures, and that he knows, "according to the Apostle, how to abound and to suffer need". To those who deny the truth of the facts narrated he asks that they produce "the originals": if they are at odds with what he writes, he will call their reproof just and will strive to amend himself.

The defence closes with the image of dust: he does not see that anything can befall him other than what befalls fine dust, which, when the whirlwind blows, is either not stirred from the ground, or is carried aloft and left "upon the heads of men, upon the crowns of kings and of emperors, and sometimes upon high palaces and upon lofty towers"; and if from there it falls, it cannot go lower than the place whence it was lifted.

## Framing and Significance

The return to the frame is marked by the formula "But return we must, since we have wandered far enough, O fair ladies, to the place whence we departed, and follow the order begun". The sun has already driven every star from the sky and from the earth "the moist shadow of night" when [[Person/filostrato]] rises and makes the whole brigata rise; in the fair garden they divert themselves, they dine where the evening before they had supped and, having risen from sleep with the sun at its highest, they sit down near the fair fountain "in the accustomed manner". Filostrato commands [[Person/fiammetta]] to make a beginning of the tales, and she, without waiting to be told anything further, begins "in womanly fashion".

The section has a particular weight in the structure of the work because it is declared in it that the *Decameron* had already circulated in part before its completion — the criticisms concern tales already read — and because in it is found the most explicit formulation of the author's poetics, opposed at once to moral and to stylistic reproof.
