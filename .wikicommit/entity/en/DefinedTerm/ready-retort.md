The **ready retort** is, in the *Decameron*, the word spoken on the spot that saves the one who utters it from an already declared danger. The rubric of [[ShortStory/chichibio-and-the-crane]] describes its effect exactly: with "a ready word to his own salvation" the protagonist turns his master's anger into laughter and escapes the ill fortune threatened him. What defines it, therefore, is not wit in itself, but the relation between the word and the risk: it intervenes when the speaker's position is already lost on the plane of facts, and reverses the outcome by operating on the plane of language.

## Usage

The tale explicitly distinguishes two possible origins of the ready retort. The first is "ready wit", which "often furnishes speakers with words prompt and useful and apt, according to the circumstances": here the retort is a faculty of the speaker. The second is fortune, "sometimes the helper of the fearful", which "suddenly places upon their tongue such words as, in a settled state of mind, the speaker could never have found". In this second case the sally is no merit of the speaker, and the narrator declares that this is precisely what she means to demonstrate: the protagonist in fact answers "without himself knowing whence it came", after having ridden "in the greatest fear in the world".

On the level of technique, an effective ready retort does not contradict the evidence that has just belied the speaker. In [[ShortStory/chichibio-and-the-crane]] it accepts the contrary proof and turns it back, shifting the question from the disputed fact to a condition that the adversary would not have satisfied: what everyone has seen is not denied; what is denied is that the experiment was conducted in the same way. It is this displacement — not the denial, but the reply that renders the denial superfluous — that produces laughter, and with laughter, deliverance.

## The Witticism as the Theme of a Day

The theme of the Sixth Day is set by [[Person/elissa]] in the Conclusion of the Fifth Day, immediately after receiving the crown from [[Person/fiammetta]]. She prefaces it by noting that it has already often been heard how "with fine witticisms or with ready retorts or with prompt precautions" many have been able "with a fitting bite to blunt the teeth of others or to drive away oncoming dangers"; and since "the matter is fair and may be useful", she establishes that the discourse shall be "of those who, being assailed by some pleasant witticism, gave as good as they got, or who by a ready retort or precaution escaped loss or danger or scorn".

The formulation distinguishes three instruments — the pleasant witticism, the ready retort and the precaution — and two outcomes to be avoided: material harm and scorn. It is moreover the only proposal of a theme that the queen justifies by usefulness as well as by the beauty of the matter.
