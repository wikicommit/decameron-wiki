Panfilo is one of the three young men who in the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]] of the [[Book/decameron]] enter the church of [[Place/santa-maria-novella]] while the seven ladies are discussing what is to be done, and he is the first of the three to be named by the text, together with [[Person/filostrato]] and [[Person/dioneo]]. None of them is less than twenty-five years old, and in none of them had the perversity of the time, the loss of friends or of kinsfolk, nor fear for themselves been able to cool, let alone to quench, love. The three were seeking, as their highest consolation amid so great a turbation of things, to see their ladies, who by chance were all three among the seven; some of the others were their close kinswomen.

Having accepted [[Person/pampinea]]'s invitation, Panfilo becomes part of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] and departs with the company on the Wednesday morning at dawn. When the queen distributes the offices, she assigns to Sirisco, Panfilo's manservant, the function of steward and treasurer, with the obligation to follow the commands of Parmeno, the seneschal.

## The first teller of tales

It having been established that the hot part of the day be passed in telling tales and that the First Day have a free theme, the queen turns to Panfilo, who sits at her right hand, and pleasantly bids him give beginning to the others with one of his tales. Having heard the command, and being listened to by all, Panfilo begins promptly: with him, therefore, opens the series of the tales of the *Decameron*.

## His reign

Panfilo governs the Tenth Day, the last of the work, and is the last of the ten to wear the crown: [[Person/emilia]] sets it upon his head, observing that he is the one "who alone remained to be honoured with such an honour", and that a great charge remains to him, "you being the last", of amending his own defects and those of all who have held that place. He receives the honour gladly and answers that her worth and that of his other subjects will bring it about that he be praised as the others have been.

Bringing the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] back "to the accustomed law" after the day of free subject granted by Emilia, he fixes the theme "of those who have wrought anything liberally, or indeed magnificently, whether in matters of love or in other matters". The motivation he gives for it is the only one of explicitly moral tenor among those of the ten sovereigns: to discourse and to act thus will kindle their spirits "to work valorously", so that "our life, which can be nothing but brief in the mortal body, shall be perpetuated in praiseworthy fame"; which everyone "who serves not the belly alone, after the manner of the beasts" must not only desire "but with every effort seek and work for".

## The return to Florence

It is Panfilo who resolves upon the dissolution of the company. At the end of the Tenth Day, having premised that to know how "to foresee things to come" is "reputed by solemn men the greatest wisdom", he observes that "tomorrow it will be fifteen days" since they left [[Place/florence]]. He draws up a reckoning of the retreat: although the tales told were merry "and perhaps enticing to concupiscence", and although they have eaten, drunk, played and sung, he has known "no act, no word, no thing" to be blamed, but "continual seemliness, continual concord, continual brotherly familiarity".

He therefore proposes the return, alleging that too long a custom might turn into tedium, that someone might cavil at their long sojourn, that each has by now had his own day and his own share of the honour, and that the brigata, "already known to several others round about", might multiply until it took from them every consolation. He declares that he wishes to keep the crown until the departure, fixed for the following morning. The counsel is taken "as useful and as seemly", and the next morning he leads the company towards Florence, where the three young men leave the seven ladies in [[Place/santa-maria-novella]].
