The *Conclusion* of the Sixth Day is the section with which the sixth day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. Two things of importance for the whole frame are accomplished in it: the crowning of [[Person/dioneo]], the only member of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] to exercise the lordship after having enjoyed for six days an exemption from its rules, and the discovery of the [[Place/valle-delle-donne]], which will become the seat of the Seventh Day.

The tale of friar Cipolla has afforded "equally to the whole brigata the greatest pleasure and diversion", and much laughter has been raised "most of all at his pilgrimage and at the relics both seen and brought back by him".

## Contents

The queen [[Person/elissa]], hearing the tale and her own lordship ended together, draws off the crown and laughingly sets it on Dioneo's head: "It is time, Dioneo, that thou shouldst prove somewhat what a burden it is to have women to rule and to guide". Dioneo, having taken the crown, answers laughingly that kings far more worthy than he have already been seen — "I mean the kings at chess" — and that he will reign as he knows how.

The theme he proposes derives avowedly from the altercation heard a few hours before: had "mistress [[Person/licisca]]" not come there a little while ago, he says, he doubts whether he would have laboured "a good while" to find matter. Leaving aside the first part of what she affirmed, "which is a childish business", he deems it pleasant to discourse upon the second, and establishes that on the morrow they shall speak "of the tricks that either for love or for their own salvation women have already played upon their husbands, whether they perceived it or not" (see [[DefinedTerm/beffa]]).

To some of the women the discoursing of such matter seems ill to befit them, and they beg him to change the proposition. The king answers with an articulated argument: he knows what he has imposed no less than they do, and their objection has not turned him from imposing it, "thinking that the time is such that, so long as both men and women guard against acting dishonourably, every discourse is permitted". He recalls that "through the perversity of this season, the judges have left the tribunals", the laws divine and human are silent, and "ample licence for the preserving of life is granted to everyone". He observes furthermore that the brigata has been "from the first day until this hour [...] most honourable" and that it will not be stained; that the honour of women is such as not to be undone even by "the terror of death"; and that indeed, whoever should know that they refuse to discourse of such things "would perhaps suspect that you were guilty in that regard". He adds finally that he has been obedient to all and does not deserve that now, having been made king, they should seek to put the law into his hand. The women consent, and the king gives leave until the hour of supper.

## Framing and Significance

The sun being still very high because the discourse has been brief, and while Dioneo and the other young men set themselves to play at tables, Elissa calls the women aside and proposes to lead them to the Valley of the Ladies, a place very near where she believes none of them has ever been. Without letting the young men hear anything, and one of the maidservants having been called, they set out; having gone little more than a mile, they reach the valley and find it as beautiful and delightful "as could ever be imagined". The heat being great and having no fear of being seen, they resolve to bathe in the little lake; the maidservant being set to guard the entrance, all seven enter it and linger there chasing the fish with their hands. Returned to the palace, [[Person/pampinea]] laughingly announces to the young men: "Today we have indeed deceived you", and recounts at length where they have been.

The king, eager to see the place, has supper promptly commanded; after which the three young men go there with their household and praise the valley "as one of the fair things of the world". Returned, the king orders the steward to have it made ready there the following morning and to carry some beds thither. Having then commanded that every man be at the dancing, and a dance being led by [[Person/panfilo]], the king turns to Elissa: since she has today done him the honour of the crown, he wishes to do her that of the song. Elissa sings with a sweet voice the ballata "Love, if I can escape thy talons" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), which invokes deliverance from the bonds of love. All marvel at such words, but none can divine what the cause of them may be. The king finally has [[Person/tindaro]] called that he may bring out his bagpipe, and to the sound of it has many dances danced, until, a great part of the night being past, he sends everyone to sleep.
