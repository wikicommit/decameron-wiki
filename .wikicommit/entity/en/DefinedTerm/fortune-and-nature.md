*Fortune* and *Nature* are, in the words of the [[Book/decameron]], *the two ministers of the world* (VI, 2, 6): the work's conception of moral life is based on their contrast. Man defines himself in relation to these two forces: one external, Fortune, which conditions him but which he can turn to his own advantage; the other internal, Nature, with instincts and appetites that he must recognise with intelligence.

## The Two Forces in the Work

In the tales Fortune often appears as an unexpected event that overturns the course of things, while Nature presents itself as a primordial force whose foremost expression is Love, an invincible feeling that governs the soul and the senses alike and that can equally be a joyous fullness of life and of death. For [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] love is an irrepressible force, a cause of delight but also of pain, which acts across the most diverse social strata and for this reason often clashes with cultural prejudices and conventions; virtue, in this context, is not the mortification of instinct, but rather the capacity to satisfy and master natural impulses.

The contrast between the two forces governs in particular the fourth day, devoted to loves *that came to an unhappy end*: these are stories in which the death of one of the lovers is inevitable because the laws of Fortune triumph over the natural laws of Love, as in the tale of [[ShortStory/lisabetta-da-messina]].

## Distance from Dante

Whereas for [[Person/dante-alighieri]] Fortune is an angelic intelligence acting within the scope of a divine plan (*Inferno*, canto VII, 76-96), the Fortune present in the *Decameron* is "chance". Boccaccio's work is not ascetic but secular, freed from the theocentrism that underlies instead the *Divine Comedy* and the medieval mentality, of which the *Decameron* represents the "autumn".
