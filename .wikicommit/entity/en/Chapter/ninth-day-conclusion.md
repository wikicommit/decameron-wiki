The *Conclusion* of the Ninth Day is the section with which the ninth day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. In it is accomplished the last crowning of the work: [[Person/panfilo]] is "he alone who remained to be honoured with such an honour", and with him the round of the sovereignty among the ten members of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] closes.

## Contents

The tales being ended and the sun already beginning to grow mild, the queen [[Person/emilia]] recognises that the end of her own lordship has come; having risen to her feet and drawn off the crown, she sets it on Panfilo's head and, smiling, tells him that a great charge remains to him, "such as is the amending of my shortcoming and of that of the others who have held the place thou holdest, thou being the last", and that God may lend him grace for it "as He has lent it to me to make thee king". Panfilo gladly receives the honour and answers that her virtue and that of his other subjects will bring it about that he shall be as praiseworthy as the others have been.

Having disposed with the steward the things needful "after the custom of his predecessors", he turns to the women. He observes that Emilia's discretion, in order to give some rest to their powers, had granted them free choice to discourse of whatever most pleased them; but, they being by now rested, he judges "that it is well to return to the accustomed law". He therefore fixes that on the morrow each think to discourse "of those who liberally or indeed magnificently performed some deed touching upon matters of love or upon anything else".

The justification he gives for it exceeds that given by the other sovereigns, and is the only one that assigns to the storytelling an explicit moral end: to discourse and to act thus will kindle "beyond any doubt" well-disposed minds to act valorously, so that "our life, which cannot be other than brief in the mortal body, will be perpetuated in praiseworthy fame"; and this everyone "who does not serve only the belly, after the fashion of beasts" ought not only to desire, "but with every diligence to seek and to work for".

## Framing and Significance

The theme pleases the glad brigata, which by the new king's leave rises and gives itself to the accustomed delights until the hour of supper. Having come to this with festivity and being served diligently and in due order, they then rise to the customary dances; and "perhaps a thousand little songs more diverting in their words than masterly in their singing" having been sung, the king commands [[Person/neifile]] that she sing one in his name. She begins without delay, "with a clear and glad voice", the ballata "I am a young maid" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), in which a young woman rejoices and sings in the new season, going through the green meadows to gather and kiss the flowers that resemble her beloved's face. The little song is much commended by the king and by all the women; and since much of the night has already gone, the king commands that each go to rest until day.

The section marks the point at which the frame prepares to close: the round of the ten sovereignties being exhausted, the following day will be the last, and the theme of magnificence that Panfilo assigns to it is the one on which the whole work will end.
