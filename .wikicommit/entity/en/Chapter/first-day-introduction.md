The *Introduction* to the First Day is the section with which the First Day of the [[Book/decameron]] of [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] opens, and which precedes the First Tale. In it the author addresses the "most gracious ladies", warning them that the work will have a "grave and wearisome beginning", since it begins with "the sorrowful remembrance of the pestiferous mortality now past"; but he compares that "horrid commencement" to a mountain harsh and steep beyond which lies a most beautiful plain, the more pleasing the greater the toil of the ascent has been. He declares that he has been led to it "as though constrained by necessity": without that recollection it could not be shown for what cause the things that follow came about.

The section divides sharply into two parts. The first is the account of the pestilence that reached Florence in 1348, conducted as a first-person testimony of what the author says he saw with his own eyes. The second stages the birth of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]]: seven young women who meet by chance in [[Place/santa-maria-novella]], Pampinea's proposal to leave the city, the objection of [[Person/filomena]] and of [[Person/elissa]], the arrival of the three young men, the departure and the settling in the country place.

The passage from the one part to the other is marked by a declaration of the author's: "It irks me myself to go on turning so much amid so many miseries", and from here he leaves aside what he can fittingly avoid in order to recount what happened while the city was "almost empty of inhabitants".

## Contents

The description of the plague occupies the first half of the section. Boccaccio dates it to the years "of the fruitful Incarnation of the Son of God", now "come to the number of one thousand three hundred and forty-eight", when into the "excellent city of Florence, most noble beyond every other Italian one" there came the deadly pestilence, begun some years earlier in the Eastern parts and spread towards the West. Against it neither the cleansing of the city ordered by the officials availed, nor the ban on the entry of the sick, nor the processions and supplications. Its signs are described — the swellings in the groin and under the armpits that the common people called "gavoccioli", then the black or livid blotches —, the swiftness of death, the forms of contagion and the dissolution of customs, of funeral rites and of family bonds, down to the mass graves and the reckoning of more than a hundred thousand dead within the walls between March and July. This matter is taken up in detail in the page devoted to the [[Event/black-death-1348]].

The second half recounts the forming of the company. On a Tuesday morning, having heard the divine offices in the "venerable church of Santa Maria Novella" and dressed in mourning, seven young women meet, all bound to one another by friendship, neighbourhood or kinship, none above twenty-eight years of age nor under eighteen. Boccaccio declares that he does not wish to report their true names, so as not to expose them to shame and not to give matter to the envious, and that he will instead name them "by names befitting the qualities of each, either wholly or in part": Pampinea, the eldest, then [[Person/fiammetta]], [[Person/filomena]], [[Person/emilia]], [[Person/lauretta]], [[Person/neifile]] and [[Person/elissa]].

There follow Pampinea's speech, Filomena's objection and Elissa's reply, the entry into the church of the three young men — [[Person/panfilo]], [[Person/filostrato]] and [[Person/dioneo]], none of them under twenty-five years of age — and the invitation addressed to them by Pampinea. The section closes with the departure on Wednesday at dawn, the arrival at the palace in the countryside, the election of the queen, the ordinance of the common life and the proposal to tell tales, after which the queen turns to Panfilo, seated on her right, that he may make a beginning with a tale of his own.

## Framing and Significance

The Introduction is the place in which the work's [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] is actually staged. In it are instituted the elements that will govern the life of the company on the days that follow: the sovereignty by rotation, whereby "the burden and the honour" falls to each for a day and whoever rules the day designates the successor as vespers draws near; the laurel garland woven by [[Person/filomena]] and set on the head of [[Person/pampinea]] as a "manifest sign" of the lordship; and the choice of storytelling, preferred by the queen to games of tables and of chess because in a game "the mind of one of the parties must needs be troubled", whereas the telling affords delight to the whole company that listens.

Pampinea's proposal is argued as a natural right: "It is natural reason, for each one who is born here, to aid and preserve and defend his life so far as he may". She sets against the city — where nothing is heard but news of the dead and the dying, where those condemned to exile run about unpunished and the gravediggers ride jeering — the countryside, where the little birds are heard singing, the hills and plains grow green, the air is fresher and the number of vexations is smaller. To Filomena's objection, that women alone do not know how to govern themselves "without the providence of some man", the answer is given in fact by fortune, which leads the three young men into the church: Pampinea recognises in it a sign favourable to their beginnings.

The section finally fixes the principle that will govern the retreat. Having become queen, Pampinea commands that no one, "wherever he go, whence he return, whatsoever he hear or see", shall bring in from outside any tidings other than glad ones: the separation from the plague-stricken city is thus sanctioned on the plane of what it is lawful to recount within the company as well.
