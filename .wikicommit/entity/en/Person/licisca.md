Licisca is one of the maidservants in the service of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] in the [[Book/decameron]]. She appears at the opening of the Sixth Day, when from the kitchen there arises "a great uproar" made by the maidservants and the manservants, and the seneschal, questioned by queen [[Person/elissa]], reports that the noise is between Licisca and [[Person/tindaro]], without knowing the cause of it.

The text describes her as "somewhat advanced in years [...] and haughty rather than not, and heated with shouting". She is the only character in the work who, not belonging to the company of the ten tellers of tales, takes the word before it and conditions what follows.

## Background

Both being led into the queen's presence, Licisca prevents Tindaro from answering first, turning towards him "with an ill countenance": "See this beast of a man, who dares, where I am, to speak before me! Let me speak". Then, turning to the queen, she sets out the matter of the quarrel: Tindaro presumes to instruct her about Sicofante's wife — as though she had not frequented her — maintaining with an obscene metaphor that she was a virgin on her wedding night; Licisca denies it, affirming the contrary.

She adds that Tindaro is beast enough to believe that young women are so foolish "that they stand losing their time waiting upon their father and their brothers", who six times out of seven delay marrying them off three or four years longer than they ought. And she concludes with the affirmation that will give matter to the following day: "I have not a neighbour who went to her husband a maid: and of the married ones too, I know well how many tricks they play upon their husbands, and what tricks; and this great sheep wants to teach me about women, as though I were born yesterday!"

While she speaks, the ladies laugh so much "that all their teeth might have been drawn out of them"; the queen imposes silence upon her "a good six times", but to no effect: Licisca does not stop until she has said what she wished.

## Role in the frame

The queen, laughing, entrusts the question to [[Person/dioneo]] that he may give "final sentence" upon it when the tales are finished; but Dioneo gives sentence immediately, without hearing more, that "Licisca is in the right" and that Tindaro "is a beast". Hearing this, Licisca laughs and turns again to Tindaro: "I said so indeed; go your way: do you think you know more than I, you, who have not yet dried your eyes? Many thanks; I have not lived here in vain, not I". Only then does the queen, with an ill countenance, impose silence upon her and command her to make no more word or noise if she does not wish to be "whipped", sending her and Tindaro away; the text observes that otherwise, for all that day, the company would have done nothing but attend upon her.

The episode does not remain without consequence. At the end of the Sixth Day, [[Person/dioneo]], having become king, declares that without the words of "mistress Licisca" he would have laboured "a good while" to find a theme to discourse upon, and fixes for the following day precisely the second part of what she had affirmed — the tricks ([[DefinedTerm/beffa]]) that women play upon their husbands — leaving aside the first, judged "childish stuff".
