The *Conclusion* of the Tenth Day is the section with which the last day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes, and with it the whole narrative frame. In it are resolved the dissolution of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] and the return to [[Place/florence]], and in it is found the reckoning that the king [[Person/panfilo]] draws of the fifteen days gone by.

The tale of [[Person/dioneo]] being ended, the women have discoursed of it at length, "some drawing one way and some another, some blaming one thing and some praising another about it", when the king, having raised his face towards the sky and seeing the sun already low at the hour of vespers, begins to speak without rising from his seat.

## Contents

Panfilo premises that the good sense of mortals consists not only in holding past things in memory or in knowing present ones, but that "by both of these to know how to foresee future things is reckoned by weighty men the greatest good sense". He then recalls the exact reckoning of the retreat: "tomorrow it will be fifteen days" since they left Florence to take some diversion for the sustaining of their health and their life, ceasing from the melancholies, the sorrows and the anguishes that are continually seen in the city "since this pestilential time began".

From this he draws a moral reckoning: in his judgement they have done it honourably, because — although glad tales have been told "and perhaps such as draw towards concupiscence", and although they have continually eaten and drunk well, played and sung, all things "apt to incite weak minds to less than honourable things" — he has known "no act, no word, no thing" to be blamed either on the women's part or on his own: it has seemed to him that he saw and heard "continual honour, continual concord, continual brotherly familiarity".

He therefore proposes the return, with three arguments: that too long a custom might breed vexation; that someone might cavil at their too long sojourn; and that, each having had their own day and their own share of the honour, it is by now "a fitting thing" to return whence they departed. He adds that the brigata, "already known round about by several others", might multiply in such a way as to take from them all consolation. He declares finally that, if they approve the counsel, he will keep the crown given him until the departure, which he intends to be the following morning; otherwise he already has ready whom to crown for the day after. After much discourse between the women and the young men, the king's counsel is taken "as useful and as honourable".

## Framing and Significance

The steward being called and having spoken with him of the manner to be kept the following morning, the king dismisses the brigata until the hour of supper. This being taken "with the utmost pleasure", they begin to sing, to play and to carol; and while [[Person/lauretta]] leads a dance, the king commands [[Person/fiammetta]] to say a song. She sings the ballata "If Love came without jealousy" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), in which she declares that she would be glad as no other woman if love did not bring jealousy with it, and prays the other women not to attempt to do her outrage in this. The song being ended, Dioneo, who is at her side, tells her laughingly that she would do "a great courtesy to make him known to all, so that through ignorance the possession might not be taken from you". Several others are then sung, and the night being already nearly half gone all go to rest.

As the new day appears, having risen and the steward having already sent away all their things, they return towards Florence "behind the guidance of their discreet king". The three young men leave the seven women in [[Place/santa-maria-novella]], whence they had set out with them, and having taken their leave of them attend to their other pleasures; and the women, when it seems to them time, return to their own houses.

The close thus brings the company back exactly to the place where the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] had formed it in the Introduction to the First Day, closing the circle of the retreat without describing what they find on their return.
