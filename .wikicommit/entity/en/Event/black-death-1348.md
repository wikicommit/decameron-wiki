The "black death" which struck [[Place/florence]] in 1348 infected the overwhelming majority of the population and is the historical event that serves as the frame of the [[Book/decameron]]. The return of [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] to Florence, between the end of 1347 and 1348, coincided with the pestilence. The epidemic spread through Europe in the year preceding that in which Boccaccio probably began to write the work.

The epidemic caused the death of many of the writer's friends — among them Sennuccio del Bene, Giovanni Villani and Jacopo Alighieri — and of numerous relatives, among whom his father and stepmother: Boccaccio thus became head of the family and guardian of his younger brother Jacopo. It was during that terrible pestilence that he elaborated the work destined to become the narrative foundation of Western storytelling.

## The description in the Introduction to the First Day

The most extended testimony on the epidemic is to be read in the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]], where Boccaccio dates it to the years "of the fruitful Incarnation of the Son of God", now "come to the number of one thousand three hundred and forty-eight", and says that it had begun some years before in the eastern parts and had spread from place to place towards the West. At Florence its effects manifested themselves "almost at the beginning of the spring" of the said year, and against it neither wisdom nor human provision availed: the city was purged of much filth by the officials deputed to it, entry was forbidden to every sick person, and much counsel was given for the preservation of health, while devout persons addressed humble supplications to God in ordered processions and in other manners.

The signs of the malady are described by contrast with what befell in the East, where the issuing of blood from the nose was a manifest sign of inevitable death: at Florence there arose instead, in men as in women, swellings in the groin or beneath the armpits, some as large as a common apple, others as an egg, which the common people called "gavoccioli"; in a brief space of time the gavocciolo began to appear indifferently in every part of the body, and the character of the infirmity changed into black or livid blotches upon the arms, upon the thighs and elsewhere, in some large and few, in others small and thick-set. The one and the other were a most certain token of death to come: almost all died within the third day from the appearance of the signs, the greater part without any fever or other accident, and against the evil neither the counsel of a physician nor the virtue of medicine seemed to avail.

The contagion is observed with particular insistence: the pestilence flung itself from the sick upon the healthy "no otherwise than fire does upon things dry or oily when they are brought very near it", and not only speaking and consorting with the sick, but even touching their clothes or any other thing touched or used by them seemed to carry it into whoever touched it. In proof of this Boccaccio relates an episode which he declares to have seen with his own eyes: the rags of a poor man dead of that infirmity, thrown into the public street, were seized with snout and teeth by two swine, which, after some tossing of them about, fell dead within a short hour upon those very rags.

## Effects upon manners and upon the city

From fear were born opposite behaviours. Some held that living moderately and guarding oneself from every superfluity could resist the evil: having formed a company, they lived separated from every other, shut up in houses where there were no sick persons, using the most delicate foods and the best wines with temperance, fleeing every luxury and refusing to hear news of the dead or of the sick. Others, of contrary opinion, affirmed that drinking freely, taking one's pleasure, going about singing and satisfying every appetite were the most certain medicine, and went from tavern to tavern by day and by night, entering also into the houses of others, become in great part common because each had abandoned his own things as though he were to live no longer. Many others held a middle way, going about without shutting themselves up and carrying in their hands flowers, odorous herbs or diverse kinds of spices to be set often to the nose, esteeming it an excellent thing to comfort the brain with such odours, since all the air seemed tainted and stinking with the stench of dead bodies, of infirmities and of medicines. Others finally, of a more cruel sentiment, judged that no medicine was better than to flee before the pestilence, and abandoned their own city, their houses, their kinsfolk and their possessions to seek out those of others, or at least their own countryside.

Meanwhile the authority of the laws, divine as well as human, was almost fallen and dissolved, because the ministers and the executors of them were dead, sick, or left so bereft of household that they could exercise no office. Fear broke the bonds of family: one brother abandoned another, the uncle the nephew, the sister the brother, and often the wife her husband; and — a greater thing and almost not to be believed — fathers and mothers shrank from visiting and serving their own children. To the sick there remained nothing but the charity of a few friends or the avarice of servants, drawn by great and unseemly wages and for the most part unskilled in such services; from this descended the usage, before scarcely ever heard of, that any woman, however graceful or gentle she might be, made no scruple of having a man in her service and of uncovering to him every part of her body as she would have done with a woman, provided the necessity of her infirmity required it.

The funeral rites too were dissolved. To the custom whereby the women of the family and the neighbourhood gathered in the house of the dead to weep, and the neighbours and other citizens gathered before the house while the clergy accompanied the body with funeral pomp of wax and of chants to the church he had chosen before his death, there succeeded new forms: many passed from this life without witnesses, and in place of weeping there were heard laughter, jests and companionable merrymaking. The bodies were accompanied at most by ten or twelve neighbours, and not by honourable citizens but by a sort of corpse-bearers "come up from among the meaner folk", who had themselves called "becchini" and rendered these services for hire, carrying the bier with hurried steps not to the church chosen by the deceased but to the nearest one. The neighbours drew the corpses from the houses and set them before the doors, where in the morning they might have been seen without number; a single bier sometimes carried two or three of them, and behind one cross with two priests there were set three or four biers, so that where the priests believed they were burying one dead man they had six or eight. The consecrated ground not sufficing, in the cemeteries of the churches there were dug enormous trenches into which those who came after were put by the hundred, stowed "as merchandise is stowed in ships, layer upon layer", and covered with a little earth until the trench was filled to the brim.

The countryside was not spared: in the scattered villages and in the fields the wretched and poor labourers and their families died without the care of a physician or the help of a servant, "not as men but almost as beasts", while oxen, asses, sheep, goats, swine, fowls and dogs, driven out of the houses, wandered through the fields where the corn had been left abandoned without even being reaped. Returning to the city, Boccaccio concludes that between the March and the following July it is believed for certain that more than a hundred thousand human creatures were taken from life within the walls of Florence, between the force of the infirmity and the fact that many sick persons were ill served or abandoned through the fear of the healthy — a number which, he observes, before the deadly accident one would perhaps not have estimated that the city contained.

## Course and consequences

The pestilence served Boccaccio as the prologue of his literary masterpiece, describing the moral misery and the death that reigned in human society. At the beginning of the work the author gives a long and detailed description of the malady that struck Florence, drawn almost entirely from personal knowledge but also from the *Historia Langobardorum* of Paul the Deacon. Besides decimating the population, the epidemic destroyed all those social norms and those usages and customs that were dear to the author.

In the *Decameron* a group of ten young people — seven girls and three young men — meet in the church of [[Place/santa-maria-novella]] during the epidemic and decide to take refuge on the hills near Florence, giving rise to the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]]. Flight from the contagion is therefore the very presupposition of the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] of the work: as Maurizio Fiorilla underlines, the tales of the honourable brigata serve to keep alive the humanity destroyed by the disease. In opposition to the paralysed city, the young people create a parallel and almost perfect reality, in demonstration that man is able to give an order to things by his own powers and his own intelligence.

The effects of the epidemic were not limited to the personal and literary sphere. According to Giovanna Frosini, the Florentine tongue used by Boccaccio for the *Decameron* is a living tongue in movement, owing also to the arrival in Florence, after the pestilence, of populations coming from other regions of Tuscany, who influenced its vocabulary and its morphology. The plague touched also the other protagonists of the culture of the time: [[Person/petrarch]] had taken refuge at Vaucluse on account of the great plague itself, and from there set out in 1350 for the Jubilee of Rome, stopping at Florence where he met Boccaccio in person for the first time.

## A sign of the plague in the countryside

Besides the description in the Introduction to the First Day, the pestilence surfaces only once in the frame of the central days, and in indirect form. In the Introduction to the Ninth Day the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]], led by the queen [[Person/emilia]] into a little wood not far from the palace, sees there the animals — "such as roebucks, stags and others" — awaiting the approach of men "no otherwise than if they had become without fear or tame"; and the text expressly gives the reason: they are "almost secure from hunters because of the pestilence that hung over all".

The remark records an effect of the epidemic upon the ordinary life of the countryside — the hunt has ceased because the hunters have ceased — within a passage otherwise idyllic. Returning to the palace garlanded with oak leaves and with their hands full of flowers, the ten young people draw from the narrator the comment which sets their gladness beside the death that surrounds them: whoever had met them "could have said nothing else but: — Either these shall not be vanquished by death, or it shall slay them merry".
