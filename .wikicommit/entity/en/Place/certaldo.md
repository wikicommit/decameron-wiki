Certaldo is the Tuscan town most closely bound to the figure of [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]], to the point that the writer was known as "il Certaldese". His birthplace is not known with exactness — Florence, Paris or Certaldo: according to [[Person/vittore-branca]], the signature "Johannes de Certaldo" used by Boccaccio would indicate the homeland of the family rather than the physical place of birth.

At Certaldo Alta stands the Casa Boccaccio. From 1363 the writer resided there for more than ten months, and from then on moved ever more rarely, so much so that the period running from 1363 to the year of his death is called the "Florentine-Certaldese period". During the fall from favour that followed the conspiracy of 1360, in the *Epistola consolatoria a Pino de' Rossi* Boccaccio compared his friend's exile to his own voluntary exile at Certaldo.

## Places and access

The historic centre, Certaldo Alta, preserves the Casa Boccaccio, today a house-museum, which houses also the seat and the library of the [[Organization/ente-nazionale-giovanni-boccaccio]]. In the church of Santi Jacopo e Filippo, at the centre of the nave, there is a tomb slab made between the end of the nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century in honour of the writer.

## History and significance

On the night of 21 December 1375 Boccaccio expired in his house at Certaldo and was buried with all honours in the church of Santi Iacopo e Filippo. Upon his tomb he wished that his dominant passion for poetry be remembered, with a Latin funerary inscription in which one reads "Genitor Bocchaccius illi, / Patria Certaldum, studium fuit alma poesis" — "Boccaccio was his father, Certaldo his homeland, the fostering art of poetry his love".

His remains did not, however, have a fitting repose: exhumed in 1783 under the cemetery laws enacted by the Grand Duke Peter Leopold, they were "discovered" at the beginning of the twentieth century by the provost of Certaldo, don Alessandro Pieratti. The bones found were identified with those of Boccaccio at the "Convegno dei dotti" of 1949, although shortly afterwards the Certaldese Giuseppe Fontanelli (1913-2004) demonstrated that the skull found together with the bones came from archaeological excavations carried out in the area.

## Certaldo in the Decameron

Certaldo is also the setting of the tenth tale of the Sixth Day of the [[Book/decameron]] (see [[ShortStory/frate-cipolla]]), which describes it from within the work. Dioneo presents it as "a castle of Valdelsa set in our own countryside, which, small though it be, was of old inhabited by noble and well-to-do men", and recalls a characteristic product of it: that soil "produces onions famous throughout all Tuscany". Upon this particular the tale founds a play of names, since the mendicant friar who goes there every year is called precisely Friar Onion (frate Cipolla) and is gladly seen by the inhabitants "perhaps no less for his name than for any other devotion".

In the same tale the castle is portrayed as an annual goal of questing — the friar found there "good pasture" for gathering alms — and its inhabitants, the Certaldesi, are the audience that flocks in a mass to the sermon to see the promised relic.
