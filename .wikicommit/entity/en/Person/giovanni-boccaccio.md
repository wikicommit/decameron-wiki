Giovanni Boccaccio, born at [[Place/certaldo]] or perhaps at [[Place/florence]] between June and July 1313 and died at Certaldo on 21 December 1375, was an Italian writer and poet, known also as "il Certaldese". He was one of the most important figures in the European literary panorama of the fourteenth century: some scholars, among them [[Person/vittore-branca]], define him the greatest European prose writer of his time, a versatile writer who amalgamated different literary tendencies and genres, bringing them together in original works under the sign of experimentalism.

His most celebrated work is the [[Book/decameron]], a collection of tales which in the following centuries was a determining element for the Italian literary tradition, above all after [[Person/pietro-bembo]] raised the Boccaccian style to a model of Italian prose in the sixteenth century. The influence of his works was not limited to the Italian cultural panorama but extended to the rest of Europe, exercising an influence upon authors such as [[Person/geoffrey-chaucer]], a key figure of English literature, or later upon Miguel de Cervantes, Lope de Vega and the Spanish classical theatre.

Together with [[Person/dante-alighieri]] and [[Person/petrarch]], Boccaccio is one of the so-called [[DefinedTerm/three-crowns]] of Italian literature. He is remembered besides as one of the precursors of humanism, whose foundations he helped to lay in the city of Florence concurrently with the activity of his friend and master Petrarch, and for having given rise to Dantean criticism and philology, devoting himself to copying codices of the *Divina Commedia* and promoting Dante's work and figure.

## Biography

Boccaccio was born of an extramarital relationship of the merchant Boccaccio di Chellino with a woman whose identity is unknown. The place of birth is not known with exactness — Florence, Paris or Certaldo: according to Vittore Branca the signature "Johannes de Certaldo", used by Boccaccio, would indicate the homeland of the family rather than the physical place of birth. Being an illegitimate son must have weighed considerably upon his sensibility: in his vernacular works he constructed a sort of mythical and idealised biography, in which he appeared as the son of a woman of the Capetian family. Recognised at a tender age by his father, around 1320 he was received into the paternal house situated in the quarter of San Piero Maggiore and made his first studies at the little school of Giovanni Mazzuoli da Strada, learning the rudiments of Latin and of the liberal arts and studying the *Divina Commedia*.

In 1327 his father took him with him to [[Place/naples]], where he acted as exchange agent for the Bardi family. Boccaccio arrived there at fourteen, passing from communal and provincial Florence to the royal and cosmopolitan court of the Angevins. After six years of apprenticeship as a merchant, his father — observing that his son preferred to devote himself to literary studies — enrolled him in law at the University of Naples; according to Natalino Sapegno, Boccaccio there followed for one academic year (1330-31) the lessons of the poet and jurist Cino da Pistoia, approaching however the poetic lessons rather than the law. He frequented the Angevin court and the rich library of King Robert of Anjou, managed by the erudite Paolo da Perugia, and there composed the [[Book/teseida]], the [[Book/filocolo]], the [[Book/filostrato]] and the *Caccia di Diana*. The tales and the adventures of merchants heard while he served at the commercial bank would be fundamental for the great narrative fresco that would come to life with the *Decameron*.

The Neapolitan period ended in the autumn of 1340, when his father recalled him to Florence on account of a severe economic problem due to the failure of several banks. Ill at ease with Florentine life, Boccaccio nevertheless sought to insert himself into the cultural life of the city with the *Comedia delle ninfe fiorentine*, the *Amorosa visione* and the *Ninfale fiesolano*; as Marco Santagata wrote, "in Florence for almost a decade Boccaccio is a private citizen". Between 1345 and 1346 he resided at Ravenna at the court of Ostasio da Polenta, bringing to completion the vernacularisation of the third and fourth decades of Livy's *Ab Urbe Condita*; in 1347 he removed to Forlì to the court of Francesco II Ordelaffi, where he composed the eclogue *Faunus*, later included in the *Buccolicum Carmen*.

Between the end of 1347 and 1348 he was compelled to return to Florence, where his return coincided with the [[Event/black-death-1348]], which caused the death of many of his friends and relatives, among them his father and stepmother, making him head of the family and guardian of his younger brother Jacopo. It was during the pestilence that he elaborated the *Decameron*, completed in 1351 — although more modern studies indicate 1355 as the year of its conclusion.

In the [[Chapter/proem]] of the *Decameron* Boccaccio speaks of himself in the first person. He declares there that he was kindled, from his earliest youth until the moment of writing, with a love "most high and noble", perhaps higher than his "low condition" would have required, and specifies that that love was to him of the greatest travail not through the cruelty of the lady loved, but through the excess of fire conceived in the mind by an ill-regulated appetite; he affirms besides that he owes to the "pleasant reasonings" and the "praiseworthy consolations" of a friend the comfort for which he believes himself not to have died. Upon this debt he founds the declared motive of the work: out of gratitude — a virtue which he says is "supremely to be commended" and whose contrary is to be blamed — he proposes in his turn to lend "some alleviation" to those who have the greatest need of it, that is to women in love.

From 1350 a profound relationship was born with Petrarch, known in person when the latter stopped three days at Florence during the Jubilee of that year. In the following years there came about gradually, according to a term coined by the Spanish philologist Francisco Rico, Boccaccio's "conversion" to the nascent humanism. In the same decade the Signoria employed him in various diplomatic embassies: in 1350 to Ravenna, to carry to Sister Beatrice, Dante's daughter, ten gold florins in the name of the captains of the company of Orsanmichele; in 1351 to persuade Petrarch to teach in the Florentine *Studium*, to stipulate an alliance with Ludwig of Bavaria and to treat with Joanna I of Naples; in 1354 to Avignon to Innocent VI; in 1359 to Milan to Bernabò Visconti. Between August 1360 and the autumn of 1362 he lodged at his own expense the Calabrian monk Leontius Pilatus, who translated the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* at Florence.

The year 1360 marked a turning point: during the elections of the priors a conspiracy was discovered in which persons close to Boccaccio had taken part and, although he was extraneous to the attempted coup d'état, until 1365 he took part in no diplomatic missions or political charges. To this period belongs the *Epistola consolatoria a Pino de' Rossi*. From 1363 he resided ever more at Certaldo, also on account of his declining health — in his last years he was afflicted by gout, scabies and dropsy. In 1362 the Carthusian monk Pietro Petroni reproved him and Petrarch for devoting themselves to the worldly pleasures of literature, a criticism that touched him so deeply as to make him think of burning his own books. Rehabilitated in 1365, he led a mission to the papal court of Avignon and in 1367 went to Rome. In his last years there formed a circle of most faithful friends at the Augustinian convent of Santo Spirito, among them Friar Martino da Signa, Benvenuto da Imola and Coluccio Salutati.

The last months passed amid physical sufferings and grief for the death of Petrarch, which occurred between 18 and 19 July 1374 and is attested by the *Epistola XXIV* addressed to the deceased's son-in-law, Francescuolo da Brossano. Boccaccio expired on the night of 21 December 1375 in his house at Certaldo and was buried in the church of Santi Iacopo e Filippo; upon his tomb he wished that his dominant passion for poetry be remembered, with a Latin funerary inscription that closes "Genitor Bocchaccius illi, / Patria Certaldum, studium fuit alma poesis".

## Works and legacy

In Boccaccio's production are distinguished the works of youth, of maturity and of old age. To the Neapolitan period belong the *Caccia di Diana* (about 1334, elsewhere 1333-1334), the *Filostrato* (1335), the *Filocolo* (1336-38, elsewhere 1336-1339) and the *Teseida* (1339-41, elsewhere 1339-1340) — the source gives their dates in forms not entirely concordant; to the second Florentine period the *Comedia delle ninfe fiorentine* (1341-1342), the *Amorosa visione* (1342-1343), the *[[Book/elegia-di-madonna-fiammetta]]* (1343-1344) and the *Ninfale fiesolano* (1344-1346). In the last twenty years he devoted himself to Latin works of erudite character — the *Genealogie deorum gentilium*, the *De Canaria* and the *De montibus* — and to others of a more popularising flavour, the *De casibus virorum illustrium* and the *De mulieribus claris*; in the vernacular there is remembered above all *Il Corbaccio* (or *Laberinto d'amore*), dated between 1363 and 1365. The corpus is completed by the *Epistole*, twenty-five letters in Latin and two in the vernacular dated between 1339 and 1374, and the *Buccolicum carmen*, a collection of sixteen eclogues.

Boccaccio was all his life a passionate devotee of Dante: he copied with his own hand three codices of the *Commedia*, wrote the *Trattatello in laude di Dante Alighieri* and held a public reading of the *Inferno* in the Badia Fiorentina, interrupted at the seventeenth canto on account of his physical collapse and flowing into the *Esposizioni sopra la Commedia*. In 1370 he transcribed besides a codex of the *Decameron*, the most celebrated [[Manuscript/hamilton-90]].

His figure represents a bridge between the Middle Ages and the modern age: Francesco de Sanctis went so far as to define him the first writer detached from the medieval mentality, while Vittore Branca, in his book *Boccaccio medievale*, tended to emphasise his medieval mentality. Gianfranco Contini glossed this by saying that "today Boccaccio appears on the one hand of medieval and retrospective culture, on the other a good Italian deuteragonist of that aristocratic movement which was Humanism". Today the principal body promoting his figure and his work is the [[Organization/ente-nazionale-giovanni-boccaccio]].
