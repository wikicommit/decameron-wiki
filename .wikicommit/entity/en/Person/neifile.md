Neifile is one of the seven young women of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]]. Her name means "the newly enamoured one" — the form *Neìfile* is also attested, rendered as "new lover" — and is a reference to the Dolce Stil Novo.

Together with [[Person/fiammetta]], [[Person/emilia]] and [[Person/lauretta]], Neifile belongs to the brigata without playing an active role: she never intervenes in the choices and may be considered a secondary figure.

## Role in the brigata

One of the principal characteristics of this character is timidity: in the introduction she blushes when [[Person/pampinea]] proposes to the young men that they join the group, and she is intimidated again when the role of queen of the Third Day is entrusted to her.

Her voice makes itself heard when she proposes to the brigata that they tell no tales on Friday, because it is the day of Christ's passion, and on Saturday, because on that day women devote themselves to the care of the body. It is from this proposal that derives the reckoning by which the time actually spent outside the city by the brigata is fourteen days rather than ten.

## Her tales

The text of the philological edition attributes to her the fourth tale of the Sixth Day, [[ShortStory/chichibio-and-the-crane]]: [[Person/lauretta]] having fallen silent and Nonna having been commended by all, the queen bids Neifile follow on. In prefacing her account the narrator enunciates the thesis it is to demonstrate, and it is the sharpest formulation the *Decameron* gives of the [[DefinedTerm/ready-retort]]: although ready wit often furnishes speakers with words useful and fair according to the circumstances, it also happens that it is fortune, "sometimes the helper of the fearful", that suddenly sets upon their tongue words which, with a mind at rest, they would never have known how to find.

## Her reign

Neifile governs the Third Day. At the end of the Second, [[Person/filomena]] places the garland upon her head, saying: "Henceforth, dear companion, let the governance of this little people be yours". The text lingers upon her blush: she becomes in the face "like a fresh rose of April or of May at the brightening of the day", with her eyes "lovely and sparkling no otherwise than the morning star" a little lowered.

Her three deliberations bear upon the calendar of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] more than those of any other sovereign. First of all she suspends the telling of tales for the Friday and the Saturday: the Friday is worthy of reverence because on it "He who died for our life suffered his passion", and on that day she deems it "a just thing and very seemly" that in honour of God one attend rather to prayers; the Saturday is the day on which women are wont to wash their heads, many fast in reverence of the Virgin, and one rests from every work in honour of the Sunday. She then disposes the removal of the company to another place, which she declares she has already "thought of and provided", to prevent "new folk coming upon us" after four days' sojourn. Finally she fixes the theme of the Third Day — "of those who by their industry have acquired something greatly desired or recovered something lost" — "saving always Dioneo's privilege".
