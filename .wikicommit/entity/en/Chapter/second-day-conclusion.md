The *Conclusion* of the Second Day is the section with which the second day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. It is the section that introduces into the calendar of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] the first interruption of the storytelling, and with it the only interval of two whole days that separates two days of tales.

The last tale, that of [[Person/dioneo]], has given "so much to laugh at to the whole company that there was none there whose jaws did not ache", and with one consent all the women declare that Dioneo spoke the truth and that Bernabò "had been a beast". The laughter over, the queen [[Person/filomena]] acknowledges that the hour is now late, that all have told their tales and that the end of her lordship has come.

## Contents

Filomena takes the garland from her head and sets it on the head of [[Person/neifile]], saying with a glad face: "Henceforth, dear companion, let the government of this little people be thine". Neifile blushes at the honour received and becomes in the face "like a fresh rose of April or of May at the breaking of day", with her eyes "charming and sparkling no otherwise than the morning star" a little lowered; her spirit recovered, and sitting somewhat higher than usual, she takes the word.

Her speech disposes three things. The first is the suspension of the storytelling: the following day is Friday and the next after it Saturday, days "somewhat tedious to most people" on account of the viands used on them; Friday, moreover, is worthy of reverence because on it "He who died for our life suffered his passion", and Neifile deems it "a just thing and a very seemly one" that in God's honour they should attend rather to prayers than to tales. Saturday is the day on which women are wont to wash their heads and take away all the dust and grime of the week gone by, and on which many fast in reverence of the Virgin and rest from every work in honour of Sunday: on that day too, then, the company will rest from storytelling.

The second disposition is the removal: since they will have remained four days in the same place, and wishing to avoid "that new folk should come upon us", Neifile deems it fitting to change and go elsewhere, and declares that she has already thought of and provided the where. The third is the theme of the Third Day, which will be more restricted than that of the second precisely because the brigata will have had more time to think: they shall speak "upon one of the many workings of fortune", and precisely "of those who by their own industry acquired something greatly desired or recovered what they had lost", saving always the [[DefinedTerm/dioneos-privilege]].

## Framing and Significance

The queen's speech being approved by all, she has the steward called and sets out to him where he is to place the tables that evening and what else he is to do for the whole time of her lordship; then she dismisses the brigata. The women and the men take the way to a little garden and, the hour of supper being come, they sup "with festivity and with pleasure"; risen from table, and [[Person/emilia]] leading the carol, [[Person/pampinea]] sings — the others responding — the ballata "What woman shall sing, if I do not sing" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), which celebrates a love requited and free of pains. Others are then sung, more dances are danced and "divers airs" are played; at last the queen judges it time to go and rest, and each withdraws with the torch before them. The two following days are devoted to the things the queen had spoken of, while the company awaits Sunday with longing.
