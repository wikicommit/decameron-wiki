The *Conclusion* of the Third Day is the section with which the third day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. In it is accomplished the passage of the crown from [[Person/neifile]] to [[Person/filostrato]], and with it the only reversal of tone in the frame: from the most licentious day of the work one passes to the one devoted to unhappy loves.

The section is also the only one in which the crowning is preceded by an exchange of quips between the outgoing queen and the incoming king, conducted upon the tales just heard.

## Contents

The tale of [[Person/dioneo]] has moved the honourable ladies to laughter "a thousand times or more". He having come to the conclusion, the queen, recognising that the term of her own lordship has arrived, takes the laurel from her head and sets it "very pleasantly" on Filostrato's, saying: "Soon we shall see whether the wolf will know how to guide the sheep better than the sheep have guided the wolves". Filostrato answers, laughing, that if he had been believed, the wolves would have taught the sheep "to put the devil back into hell no worse than Rustico did to Alibech", and that therefore they should not call him a wolf where they are not sheep. Neifile retorts by recalling the tale of *Masetto da Lamporecchio*: had they wished to teach the women, the men might rather have learned good sense as Masetto learned it from the nuns. Filostrato, "knowing that scythes were to be found no less than he had arrows", leaves off the jesting and gives himself to the government of the kingdom, having the steward called and ordering what befits the duration of his lordship.

Turning to the women, Filostrato justifies the theme by his own condition: "through my misadventure", he says, ever since he knew good from ill he has always been subject to Love through the beauty of one or another of them, and neither being humble nor being obedient nor following him has availed him to avoid being abandoned and going "always from bad to worse"; and so he believes it will go until his death. For this reason it does not please him that tomorrow anything be spoken of save that "which is most conformable to my own case, that is, of those whose loves had an unhappy end" — adding that it was for no other reason that "the name by which you call me was laid upon me by one who well knew what he was saying". This said, he dismisses everyone until the hour of supper.

## Framing and Significance

The garden is so beautiful and delightful that none chooses to go out of it to seek pleasure elsewhere: some set themselves to follow the roe deer, the rabbits and the other animals that, leaping among them, had come perhaps a hundred times to trouble them. Dioneo and [[Person/fiammetta]] begin to sing "of messer Guiglielmo and of the Lady of Vergiù", [[Person/filomena]] and [[Person/panfilo]] give themselves to playing at chess. The tables being set about the fair fountain, they sup there "with the greatest delight".

The tables cleared, Filostrato — "so as not to depart from the path kept by those who had been queens before him" — commands [[Person/lauretta]] to lead a dance and sing a song. She excuses herself, saying that she knows no songs of others' making nor has any of her own in mind "that is very fitting to so glad a brigata", and the king answers that nothing of hers could be other than fair. Lauretta then sings, "with a voice very sweet, but in a manner somewhat piteous" and with the others responding, the ballata "No woman disconsolate" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), the lament of a woman fair and unrequited, passed from a lover who held her dear to a jealous youth.

The song is noted by all and "was differently understood by different people": there were those who chose to understand it "in the Milanese fashion, that a good pig is better than a fair lass", while others were "of a more sublime and better and truer understanding", of which the text declares that "it is not fitting to recite it at present". The king then has many double torches lit upon the grass and the flowers and has several more songs sung, until every star begins to fall; it then seeming to him time to sleep, he commands that each return with a good night to their own chamber.
