Florence is the city in which [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] passed his childhood and a great part of his adult life, and in which the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] of the [[Book/decameron]] is set. Recognised by his father at a tender age, around 1320 the young Boccaccio was received into the paternal house situated in the quarter of San Piero Maggiore.

Compared with Angevin [[Place/naples]], seat of a royal and cosmopolitan court, Florence was a strongly provincial communal city: on his return from his Neapolitan sojourn, in the autumn of 1340, Boccaccio showed himself impatient of a life he felt to be too narrow, and sought all his life to return to his beloved Naples. As Marco Santagata wrote, "in Florence for almost a decade Boccaccio is a private citizen".

## Geography and places

In the *Decameron* the ten young people of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] meet in the church of Santa Maria Novella and decide to take refuge on the hills near Florence. Other Florentine places bound to Boccaccio are the basilica of Santo Spirito, with the adjoining Augustinian convent, which in his last years was a meeting place for the intellectuals close to the humanistic sensibility and housed the so-called "Parva libreria", the body of books which Boccaccio gave to Martino da Signa according to his testamentary wishes; and the Badia Fiorentina, where the writer held the public reading of Dante's *Commedia*, interrupted at the seventeenth canto of the *Inferno*. In the Galleria degli Uffizi is preserved the portrait of Boccaccio painted by Andrea del Castagno in 1450 as a detail of the *Cycle of Illustrious Men and Women*.

## History and significance

In 1348 the city was struck by the [[Event/black-death-1348]], which infected the overwhelming majority of the population; it was during that pestilence that Boccaccio elaborated the *Decameron*. According to Giovanna Frosini, the arrival at Florence after the pestilence of populations coming from other regions of Tuscany influenced the vocabulary and the morphology of the Florentine tongue which Boccaccio adopted for the work.

The Signoria employed Boccaccio several times in diplomatic embassies, being well aware of his rhetorical qualities, charging him among other things in 1351 to persuade [[Person/petrarch]] to settle in the city to teach in the newly born *Studium*. It was at Florence that Boccaccio helped to lay the foundations of humanism, concurrently with the activity of his friend and master Petrarch, and that he obtained from the Signoria the reception of Leontius Pilatus into the *Studium* as teacher of Greek. In humanistic Florence, indebted to the philological lesson imparted by Boccaccio to the young pupils of the Santo Spirito circle, there took shape — together with the biographies of Dante and Petrarch written by Leonardo Bruni — the cult of the Florentine [[DefinedTerm/three-crowns]].
