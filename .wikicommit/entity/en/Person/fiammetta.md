Fiammetta is one of the ten young people of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]] and is at the same time the name by which [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] idealised his own inspiring muse in many of his works. It is a *senhal*, that is to say a fictitious name, a device used by the Provençal poets to address the beloved lady while concealing her true identity. The name alludes to the flame, that is to the ardour of the young woman, and is among the most used in Boccaccio's works, appearing in the [[Book/filocolo]], in the [[Book/teseida]], in the [[Book/elegia-di-madonna-fiammetta]], in the *Commedia delle Ninfe Fiorentine* and in the *Rime*.

## Fiammetta as narrator in the Decameron

In the *Decameron* Fiammetta is the queen of the Fifth Day. According to the encyclopaedic entry on the work it is not by chance that she occupies the fifth position in all of Boccaccio's works: this would be an allusion to Maria, the author's muse, whose name is composed of five letters. The central subject in the tales she tells is love, both tragic and happy, but also betrayal, present in the sixth tale of the Third Day, which is committed by a married woman.

Together with [[Person/neifile]], [[Person/emilia]] and [[Person/lauretta]], Fiammetta belongs to the brigata without, however, playing an active role: she never intervenes in the group's choices and may be considered a secondary figure with respect to [[Person/pampinea]], [[Person/filomena]] and [[Person/elissa]].

## In the Introduction to the First Day

Fiammetta appears among the seven young women who meet in [[Place/santa-maria-novella]], and is the second whom [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] names in the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]], immediately after [[Person/pampinea]]. Here too the name is not the real one: the author declares that he wishes to name them "by names suited wholly or in part to the qualities of each", so that shame may not come to them in future from the things told and heard, and so as not to give matter to the envious.

In the day spent at the palace in the countryside Fiammetta appears at two moments. When, the tables cleared after the meal, the queen has the instruments brought, it is she who takes up a viol while [[Person/dioneo]] takes a lute: together they begin sweetly to play a dance, to which the queen and the other ladies, with two of the young men, take up a round. Shortly before, in distributing the offices among the servants, Pampinea had assigned Stratilia, Fiammetta's maidservant, together with Chimera, [[Person/lauretta]]'s maidservant, to the governance of the ladies' chambers and to the cleanliness of the place.

## The meeting and the literary myth

Having become a cultivated and enthusiastic autodidact, the poet created his own literary myth according to the dictates of the Stilnovist tradition. The first meeting took place in the basilica of San Lorenzo Maggiore in [[Place/naples]], on 30 March 1336: at the "fourth hour of the day", while in the church the rites of Holy Saturday were being celebrated in the presence of the king, the queen and all the dignitaries of the Angevin court, the poet's eyes met those of a noblewoman of "marvellous beauty".

The details of the first meeting are gathered from Boccaccio himself, who in the [[Book/filocolo]] states that the meeting took place on the Saturday ("a day whose first hour Saturn had ruled") of Holy Week and in "a gracious and fair temple in Parthenope, named after him who, to be deified, endured that sacrifice be made of him upon the gridiron", that is in the basilica dedicated to San Lorenzo. According to Boccaccio's account the love story went on for three years, until the lady fell in love with another man, abandoning the poet to his torment of love.

## The question of the identification

The historical school of the late nineteenth century identified Fiammetta with Maria d'Aquino, illegitimate daughter of Robert of Anjou and of his Provençal mistress Sibila Sabran; from Boccaccio too it is learned that the lady descended from the family of Saint Thomas and that her mother's stock came from "togaed Gaul". Recent scholarship, in particular that of Lucia Battaglia Ricci and Marco Santagata, has however definitively disproved this identification: Santagata writes clearly that the "Maria d'Aquino" described by Boccaccio is a "literary phantom". Battaglia Ricci, while denying the identification, leaves open the supposition that behind the figure there was a woman who really existed, observing that she "was not the fabled Maria d'Aquino, illegitimate daughter of King Robert, but neither can she have been a myth created entirely by the poet's imagination".

## Her reign

Fiammetta governs the Fifth Day. At the end of the Fourth, [[Person/filostrato]], having excused himself for the matter he had imposed, places the laurel "upon her most golden head", declaring that he gives it to her as to one who will know better than any other how to console her companions for the harsh day just passed. The text gives at this point the most minute portrait in the work: her hair "curling, long and of gold" falling upon her white and delicate shoulders, her rounded face "all resplendent with a true colour of white lilies and vermilion roses mingled together", two eyes "that seemed a peregrine falcon's" and "a little mouth whose lips seemed two small rubies".

She accepts with a smile and immediately overturns the theme of the day just concluded: "so that you may the better perceive what you have done", she commands that each prepare to discourse "of what befell happily to some lover, after fierce or ill-starred mishaps".

In the Introduction to the Fifth Day it is she who, urged by the birdsong of the first hour, rises first and has her companions and the three young men called, leading them to disport themselves through the broad plain upon the dewy grass. In the afternoon she seats herself beside the fountain *pro tribunali* and, looking towards [[Person/panfilo]], smiling bids him give beginning "to the happy tales".
