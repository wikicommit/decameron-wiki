Giotto is the Florentine painter who appears as protagonist of the fifth tale of the Sixth Day of the [[Book/decameron]] (see [[ShortStory/forese-and-giotto]]), where [[Person/panfilo]] presents him as an example of "marvellous wits" laid up by nature "beneath most ugly forms of men".

Of his wit the text says that it was "of such excellence that there is nothing given by nature, mother of all things and worker with the continual turning of the heavens, that he did not paint with the stylus and with the pen or with the brush so like to it that it seemed not like it, but rather the thing itself"; so much so that in his works "many times [...] it is found that men's sense of sight was deceived thereby, believing that to be true which was painted".

## Background

The tale places him among the citizens of Florence and attributes to him possessions in the Mugello, which he went to see in person; it also reports that he was "a most fine talker", and that as to appearance he was "in nothing more handsome, either in person or in aspect" than the jurist messer Forese da Rabatta, his travelling companion in the tale.

In the episode narrated, returning towards Florence from the Mugello under a summer downpour, ill clad in a borrowed old cloak and muddied by the splashings of his nag, Giotto answers Forese's jesting about his own scrawny appearance with the retort that gives the tale its outcome: a stranger meeting them would believe Giotto the best painter in the world at the very moment when, looking at Forese, he would credit him with a knowledge of the ABC.

## Works & Achievements

The text names no individual works, but formulates an overall judgement upon his historical role: Giotto, "having [...] brought back into the light that art which for many centuries had lain buried beneath the errors of certain men who painted rather to delight the eyes of the ignorant than to please the understanding of the wise, may deservedly be called one of the lights of Florentine glory".

To this praise the tale adds a note upon his character. His greatness was the more meritorious to him "the greater the humility with which he acquired it, living therein the master of the others, yet always refusing to be called master"; and the title refused by him shone in him the more brightly the more greedily it was usurped by those who knew less of it than he, or by his own disciples.
