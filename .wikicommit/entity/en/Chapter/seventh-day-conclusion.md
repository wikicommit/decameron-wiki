The *Conclusion* of the Seventh Day is the section with which the seventh day of the [[Book/decameron]] closes, the only one spent entirely in the [[Place/valle-delle-donne]]. In it is accomplished the passage of the crown from [[Person/dioneo]] to [[Person/lauretta]], and in it is disposed the second and last interruption of the storytelling in the calendar of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]].

## Contents

Zephyr has risen "by reason of the sun that was drawing near to the west" when the king, his own tale being ended and no other remaining to be told, takes the crown from his head and sets it upon Lauretta's, saying: "Madam, I crown you with yourself queen of our brigata". Having become queen, Lauretta has the steward called and lays it upon him to have the tables set in the pleasant valley "somewhat earlier than is usual", so that afterwards they may return at leisure to the palace.

Turning to the company, she enunciates the theme with a quip: Dioneo wished that today they should discourse of the tricks that women play on their husbands, and were it not that she does not wish to show herself "of the breed of the cur, which will at once avenge itself", she would say that tomorrow they should discourse of the tricks that men play on their wives. Leaving this aside, she establishes instead that each think to tell "of those tricks that all day long either woman plays on man or man on woman or the one man on the other" (see [[DefinedTerm/beffa]]), believing that the discourse will be no less pleasant than that of the day just concluded. This said, she dismisses the brigata until the hour of supper.

## Framing and Significance

The women and the men having risen, some begin to go barefoot through the clear water, others divert themselves among the fair and straight trees upon the green meadow. Dioneo and [[Person/fiammetta]] sing together a good while "of Arcita and of Palemone". Supper being come, and having seated themselves at table along the little lake, they sup "restfully and with gladness" to the song of a thousand birds, refreshed by a gentle breeze and "without a single fly". The tables being cleared and the valley circled once more, the sun being still high at mid-vespers, they take up again at a slow pace the way towards their accustomed dwelling, and reach the fair palace "very near nightfall".

The weariness of the short journey being driven away with the coolest wines and comfits, they dance about the fair fountain, now to the sound of [[Person/tindaro]]'s bagpipe and now to other airs. At last the queen commands [[Person/filomena]] to say a song, and she sings the ballata "Alas! wretched my life!" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), whose longing to find the beloved again makes the whole brigata judge "that a new and pleasing love held Filomena fast"; and since from the words it appears that she had felt more than mere sight, some of them bear her envy for it, holding her the happier on that account.

Recalling finally that the following day is Friday, Lauretta recalls that it is consecrated to the passion of the Lord and that the brigata kept it devoutly when [[Person/neifile]] was queen, abstaining then on the following Saturday as well; wishing to follow "the good example given us by Neifile", she establishes that tomorrow and the day after they shall likewise abstain from the delightful storytelling, calling to mind "what befell on such days for the salvation of our souls". The devout speech pleases everyone, and the company, dismissed, goes to rest.
