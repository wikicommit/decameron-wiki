**Friendship** — *amistá* in the language of the text — is, in the eighth tale of the Tenth Day of the [[Book/decameron]], the elective bond between two persons, opposed by election to the bond of blood: "friends we have such as we choose for ourselves, and kinsmen such as fortune gives us", says Titus in the oration with which he defends Gisippus, adding that "the bond of friendship binds far more closely than that of blood or kinship". In the oration in the temple Titus presents it as a duty — Gisippus, he says, "has done what a friend ought to do" — and invokes "the holy laws of friendship"; in the story this duty is invoked against marriage contracts, against the judgement of kinsmen and finally against one's own self-preservation.

## Use in the Tale

Gisippus enunciates two principles that govern the bond. The first concerns confidence: dishonourable matters are no less to be disclosed to a friend than honourable ones, because "he who is a friend, as he takes pleasure with his friend in honourable things, so he strives to remove the dishonourable ones from his friend's mind" — a reproach he addresses to Titus for having long kept his passion hidden from him. The second concerns goods: since they became friends, he does not recall having had anything "that was not as much yours as mine", and on this basis he treats his own bride as a common good to be disposed of. In support he adds an explicit calculation: since he could easily find another wife but not another friend, he prefers to transfer the woman "from good to better" rather than lose Titus.

In the speech delivered by Titus in the temple, the same notion becomes a public argument. He declares himself content merely to have reminded his hearers that the bond of friendship binds more closely than that of blood, and infers from this that no one should marvel if Gisippus loved his friend's life more than the goodwill of his kinsmen. The final reversal of the story puts the reciprocity of the bond to the test: Titus, recognising his friend now reduced to beggary and condemned to the cross, finds no other way than to accuse himself of the murder, and Gisippus, though recognising that Titus acts for his salvation "as one grateful for the service already received from him", weeping persists in his own confession, saying that "Titus's pity comes now too late for my deliverance".

## Concluding Encomium

The tale closes with an explicit encomium that serves as a definition of the term: friendship is "a most holy thing", worthy not only of singular reverence but of perpetual praise, "the most discerning mother of magnificence and of honesty, sister of gratitude and of charity, and enemy of hatred and of avarice", ever ready, without waiting to be entreated, to perform virtuously in another what it would wish to be performed in itself. The link with [[DefinedTerm/magnificence]] is thus affirmed by the text itself, which makes the former the mother of the latter.

The encomium is accompanied by a lament over the present: its "most sacred effects" are today very rarely seen in two persons, to the fault and shame of the "wretched cupidity of mortals", which, looking only to its own advantage, has relegated it to perpetual exile beyond the farthest bounds of the earth. A series of rhetorical questions then retraces the gestures of the tale — the yielding of the bride, Gisippus's abstinence, his indifference to the murmuring of the people, the rush to his own death to take his friend down from the cross, the sharing of the patrimony, the granting of his sister — asking for each what love, what riches, what kinship, what law or what fear could have produced it "if not she". The close sets against the friend the multitude of consorts, of brothers, of sons and of servants, of whom it is observed that each fears every least danger of his own more than he cares to remove the great dangers of his father, his brother or his lord.
