Elissa is one of the seven young women of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]]. Her name is the other name of Dido, the queen of Virgil's *Aeneid*, and represents tragic love; it is not by chance that Elissa narrates the fourth tale of the Fourth Day alluding to the fourth book of the *Aeneid*, in which the story of Dido abandoned is told, even though no affinity emerges between the two figures.

Together with [[Person/pampinea]] and [[Person/filomena]], Elissa stands out within the work for her own sense of initiative.

## Role in the brigata

In the introduction Elissa takes the word immediately after Filomena's intervention and makes clear that a woman is not free to take any decision without the presence of a man. She is also the one who firmly rebukes [[Person/dioneo]] at the end of the Fifth Day, to prevent him from introducing obscene themes into the ballata.

Notable is the role she plays in the conclusion of the Sixth Day: it is Elissa herself who leads the ladies alone into the so-called "Valley of the Ladies", an idyllic place surrounded by mountains, rivers and little lakes, where they, taking advantage of the heat, decide to bathe in those crystalline waters. Many scholars have reflected upon the attribution of the place to the women: the immersion in the waters may be seen as an act of purification from the plague, or from the deception committed against the men, since the women went into the valley alone. According to this reading it would not be by chance that deception then becomes the central theme of the tales of the Seventh Day. The text of the philological edition now makes it possible to specify in what sense: in the Conclusion of the Sixth Day [[Person/dioneo]], having become king, fixes as the theme "the tricks which, either for love or for their own salvation, women have already played upon their husbands, whether the husbands perceived them or no", and he draws it avowedly from the words of the maidservant [[Person/licisca]]. These are therefore the tricks played by women upon their husbands, not those played by men upon women, as is nevertheless read in another passage of the encyclopaedic entry.

## Her tales

In all the tales told by Elissa obscene themes are never used: at the centre of her stories is the image of an adulterous woman who uses her wit for fear of being discovered by her husband, but also the theme of a marriage obstructed by one of the parents for social reasons.

The text of the philological edition attributes to her the third tale of the Eighth Day, [[ShortStory/calandrino-and-the-heliotrope]]: a tale of [[Person/panfilo]] having ended, at which the ladies had laughed so much "that they laugh still", the queen commits it to Elissa to follow on, and she begins "still laughing", saying that she does not know whether she will succeed in making them laugh as much with a little tale of hers "no less true than pleasing". It is therefore to her that the *Decameron* entrusts one of the most expansive realisations of the [[DefinedTerm/beffa]].

## Her reign

Elissa governs the Sixth Day. At the end of the Fifth, [[Person/fiammetta]] sets the crown of laurel upon her head, saying: "To you, my lady, it now falls to command". Having given order with the seneschal for whatever is needed, she fixes the theme, observing that it has already been heard many times how "with fair witticisms, or with ready answers, or with quick forethought" many have known how "to blunt others' teeth or to drive away oncoming perils"; and since the matter is fair and may be useful, she establishes that they discourse "of those who, being assailed by some graceful witticism, have requited it, or who by a ready answer or by forethought have fled loss or peril or scorn" (see [[DefinedTerm/ready-retort]]).

Her day opens with the only episode in which the servants burst in before the company: having heard "a great uproar" from the kitchen, Elissa has [[Person/licisca]] and [[Person/tindaro]] brought and asks the cause of the quarrel. She imposes silence upon Licisca in vain "a good six times", laughingly entrusts the question to [[Person/dioneo]] that he may give sentence upon it, and finally, "with an ill countenance", threatens Licisca to have her "whipped" if she says another word, and sends them both away.

It is Elissa, moreover, who discovers the [[Place/valle-delle-donne]] to her companions: at the end of the Sixth Day, while the young men are playing at tables, she calls the ladies aside and proposes to lead them thither, saying that she has desired to do so ever since they arrived and had not before today seen a fitting occasion. That same evening, Dioneo having been crowned, it is to her that the new king renders "the honour of the song": she sings with sweet voice the ballata "Amor, s'io posso uscir de' tuoi artigli", at whose words all marvel without being able to divine the cause.
