Francesco Petrarch, called also the Aretine, was the man of letters whom [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] considered all his life a *praeceptor*, able to raise him up from the sins of the flesh by means of classical literature and Augustinian spirituality, to the point of considering him a veritable spiritual guide. With [[Person/dante-alighieri]] and Boccaccio he is one of the [[DefinedTerm/three-crowns]] of Italian literature.

## Biography

Boccaccio heard Petrarch spoken of already during the Neapolitan sojourn, thanks to Father Dionigi da Borgo Sansepolcro — who arrived at Naples in 1338 — and perhaps to Cino da Pistoia, thus having news of this prodigious young man residing at Avignon. Having returned to [[Place/florence]], his acquaintance with Sennuccio del Bene and other Florentine admirers, among them the proto-humanists Lapo da Castiglionchio, Francesco Nelli, Bruno Casini, Zanobi da Strada and Mainardo Accursio, gave birth in Boccaccio to an interest and a profound admiration for him.

The meeting in person came about on the occasion of the Jubilee of 1350, when Petrarch left Vaucluse — where he had taken refuge on account of the great plague — to go to Rome: in agreement with the circle of Florentine friends, he decided to stop three days at Florence to read and expound his works. It was a moment of extraordinary intensity: Lapo da Castiglionchio gave Petrarch Quintilian's *Institutio oratoria*, while Petrarch afterwards sent them the *Pro Archia*, discovered years before in the library of the abbey of Saint James at Liège.

The relationship passed through a crisis in 1353-54, when Petrarch refused the invitation of the Commune of Florence to assume the role of teacher in the newborn *Studium* and accepted instead that of Giovanni II Visconti, bitterest enemy of the Florentines. The crisis being overcome, in 1355 Boccaccio gave his friend two most precious codices: one of Saint Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, followed shortly after by the one containing Varro's *De Lingua Latina* and Cicero's *Pro Cluentio*. Petrarch died between 18 and 19 July 1374; Boccaccio had met him for the last time in 1368, when the poet was by then settled at Arquà.

## The relationship with Boccaccio

For his part the Aretine intellectual nourished a sort of intellectual detachment from his affectionate friend, although he considered him the only one "to be his companion in the titanic cultural undertaking he was accomplishing". Petrarch never allowed Boccaccio to gain full access to his personal library, while the latter procured for him rare codices containing Latin works and the versions from the Greek prepared by Leontius Pilatus. Irritated by Boccaccio's excessive concern for his health, he decided to translate into Latin the last tale of the [[Book/decameron]], that of Griselda, to demonstrate his vigour once more. The relationship was not, however, one of "psychological subjection", but rather a "proud vindication of the part sustained by him so that the global project conceived by Petrarch might prevail"; nor was it only Petrarch who was Boccaccio's *praeceptor*, since the latter too left a significant imprint upon Petrarch's production.

The two found themselves in disagreement upon at least two questions. On the "Greek question", Petrarch held that the whole heritage of Greek culture had been absorbed by the Latin, while Boccaccio — in close contact with Leontius Pilatus's work of translation — held that the Latins had not absorbed all the notions of Hellenic civilisation. On the "Senecan question", the *Epistola XX* written to the Neapolitan jurist Pietro Piccolo da Monteforte reveals Boccaccio's bitterness at having been contradicted by Petrarch upon the existence of two distinct Senecas: although the philological procedures adopted by Boccaccio proved exact, Petrarch held, on the basis of the practically identical style, that they could not be two distinct authors.
