The *Conclusion* of the First Day is the section with which the first of the ten days of the [[Book/decameron]] closes. The sun has already declined towards vespers and the heat is for the most part abated when the tales "of the young women and of the three young men" are found to be ended, and the queen [[Person/pampinea]] announces that nothing more remains for her rule to do "save to give you a new queen".

The section has a structural importance greater than its brevity: it is here that the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] passes from the simple free storytelling of the first day to the regulated framework that will govern all the following ones. Three institutions of the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] are in fact established in it — the succession of the crown, the obligatory theme, and the exception reserved to a single storyteller.

## Contents

Pampinea first gives the reason for her choice of the hour: although the day seems to last until night, whoever does not take "some little time in advance" cannot provide for what is to come, and it is needful that whatever the new queen shall determine for the following morning may be made ready; from now on the days will begin at this hour. She then designates [[Person/filomena]], a "most discreet young woman", and rising sets the laurel garland on her head; Filomena, "somewhat blushing for shame", confirms the offices already assigned by Pampinea and orders what is to be done for the following morning and supper.

Filomena then declares that she does not wish to follow only her own judgement but that of the company as well, and judges that she will not change the manner kept by Pampinea until it becomes tedious "either through too much continuance or for some other cause". She introduces, however, the novelty that her predecessor had been prevented from adopting by being "elected late to the rule": to restrict the matter of the storytelling "within some bounds" and to make it known in advance, so that each may have room to think of a fine tale "upon the given proposition". The theme fixed for the Second Day is: "those who, harried by divers things, have come beyond their hope to a happy end".

The ordinance being approved by all, [[Person/dioneo]] asks for and obtains a gift valid "for so long as our company shall last": not to be constrained to tell a tale according to the given proposition, but the one that shall best please him, offering in exchange to be always the last to discourse (see [[DefinedTerm/dioneos-privilege]]). The queen, who knows him for a "diverting and festive man", divines that he asks it only so as to be able to cheer the brigata with a tale to laugh at should it be weary of discoursing, and with the consent of the others grants him the favour.

## Framing and Significance

The company then goes towards "a stream of most limpid water" that descends from a little mountain into a shady valley, and there they linger barefoot and with bare arms in the water; returned to the palace, they sup and give a beginning to the dances. By the queen's command [[Person/lauretta]] leads a dance and [[Person/emilia]] sings, assisted by Dioneo's lute, the ballata "I am so enamoured of my own beauty" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]), whose theme is delight in one's own beauty beheld in the mirror, which makes every other love superfluous. The little ballata being ended, "to which all had gladly responded, though it made some of them think much upon its words", and some other little carols having been danced, it pleases the queen to give an end to the first day: the torches being lit, each withdraws to their own chamber.
