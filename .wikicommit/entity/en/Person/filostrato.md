Filostrato is the second of the three young men named in the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]] of the [[Book/decameron]], between [[Person/panfilo]] and [[Person/dioneo]]. He enters with the other two into the church of [[Place/santa-maria-novella]] while the seven ladies are discoursing upon the way to provide for their own safety; the text specifies that none of the three is less than twenty-five years old, that all three are "each of them pleasing enough and well-mannered", and that each of them loves one of the seven ladies there present — a circumstance which [[Person/neifile]], blushing, fears may expose the company to infamy and reproof, but which [[Person/filomena]] judges to be of no consequence where one lives honestly.

Invited by [[Person/pampinea]] in the name of all, the three young men at first believe themselves to be mocked, but when they see that the lady speaks in earnest they answer gladly that they are ready and, giving no delay to the undertaking, give order for what is needed for the departure. Filostrato thus becomes part of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] that leaves the city on the Wednesday at dawn.

## In the governance of the company

When Pampinea, made queen, distributes the offices among the manservants of the three young men and the maidservants of the ladies, she assigns to Tindaro the service of Filostrato and of the other two, with the task of attending upon their chambers whenever the others, being hindered about their own offices, cannot do so.

## His reign

Filostrato governs the Fourth Day, and is the first man to wear the crown. At the end of the Third Day [[Person/neifile]] places it upon his head with a witticism: "We shall soon see whether the wolf will know better how to guide the sheep than the sheep have guided the wolves". He answers laughing in kind, but at Neifile's rejoinder — which reminds him of the tale of *Masetto da Lamporecchio* — "recognising that there were sickles to be found no less than he had arrows", he lets the jesting be and gives himself to the governance of the realm.

He is the only sovereign to motivate the theme by his own personal history. "By my misadventure", he says, ever since he knew good from evil he has always been subject to Love for the beauty of one or another of the ladies, and neither being humble nor being obedient nor following him has availed to keep him from being abandoned and from going "ever from bad to worse"; and thus he believes it will go until his death. For this reason he establishes that they discourse "of those whose loves had an unhappy end", adding that not for anything else was "the name by which you call me laid upon me by one who knew well what he was saying".

At the end of his own day he excuses himself "with pleasant enough words" for having made them discourse "of matter so fierce as is that of the unhappiness of lovers", and crowns [[Person/fiammetta]] as her who will best know how to console her companions. The queen then commands him to sing, being certain "that your songs are such as your tales are": he sings the ballata "Lagrimando dimostro", whose words show "clearly enough what Filostrato's mind was, and the cause of it".
