The *Valley of the Ladies* is the valley to which the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]] goes at the end of the Sixth Day and in which it passes the whole of the Seventh Day. The text presents it as a place already existing and already so named: [[Person/elissa]], proposing to her companions to lead them thither, says that it lies "in a part quite near to this place, where I do not believe that any of you was ever, and it is called the Valley of the Ladies".

It is a little more than a mile distant from the palace, and one enters it "by a very narrow way", along one side of which runs a most limpid little river. It is the most minute description of natural landscape in the work, and the text expressly attributes its source to a reported testimony: "according to what one of them afterwards told me".

## Geography and access

The floor of the valley is "as round as if it had been made with compasses", though it seems the artifice of nature and not of hand, and its circuit is a little more than half a mile. Six little hills of no great height surround it, upon the summit of each of which is seen "a palace made almost in the form of a fair little castle". The slopes descend towards the floor "as we see in theatres the steps come successively ordered from their summit down to the lowest, ever narrowing their circle".

The exposure governs the vegetation: the slopes turned towards the south are all of vines, olives, almonds, cherries, figs and other kinds of fruit-bearing trees, "without a span of them being lost"; those turned towards the north are little woods of small oaks, ashes and other trees "most green and as straight as they could possibly be". The floor, which has no other entrance save that by which the ladies came thither, is full of firs, cypresses, laurels and pines "so well composed and so well ordered as if whoever is the best craftsman in that art had planted them", among which little or no sun penetrates to the ground; this last is all a meadow of the finest grass, full of purple flowers and of others of divers colours.

A little river falls down "by ledges of living rock" from one of the valleys that divide two of the little hills, making in its fall "a sound most delightful to hear" and spraying in such a manner that from afar it seems quicksilver. Gathered into a fair little channel, it runs most swiftly to the middle of the floor and there forms a small lake, such as the townsfolk sometimes make "by way of a fishpond" in their gardens. The little lake is no deeper than a man is tall up to the breast; it is most clear, shows a bottom of "the finest gravel" that might be counted one by one, and there is seen to move in it so much fish "that beyond the delight it was a marvel". It has no other bank than the soil of the meadow, and the overflowing water is received by another little channel which issues from the little valley towards the lower parts.

## History and significance

The seven women come thither at the end of the Sixth Day, without letting the young men hear anything of it, while these are playing at tables. The heat being great and having no fear of being seen, they resolve to bathe: they command their maidservant to remain above the way of entrance and to warn them if anyone should come, and all seven enter the little pool, whose water "no otherwise hid their white bodies than a thin glass would hide a vermilion rose". There they chase the fish, seeking to take them with their hands, take some of them, then dress themselves again and return to the palace. It is [[Person/pampinea]] who tells the king [[Person/dioneo]], laughing, where they have been and what they have done; and the king, desirous of seeing the place, has the supper hastened, after which the three young men go thither with their servants, praise it "for one of the fair things of the world", bathe there and return home late.

The king then orders the steward to have all made ready there for the following morning, and to carry thither some beds for those who might wish to sleep or to lie down at midday. The Seventh Day therefore takes place there in its entirety: the steward goes thither at dawn with a great baggage-train, the brigata follows him before the rays of the sun are well risen, and the valley appears to them the more beautiful than on the preceding day "by as much as the hour of the day was more conformable to its beauty". The echo too is recorded there: as they sing, "the valley together with them" repeats the same songs, while the birds, "as though they would not be outdone", add to them "sweet and new notes". The tables are set beneath the lively laurels near the little lake, and as they eat they see the fish swimming "in very great shoals"; then beds are made in several places of the little valley, girt and enclosed by the steward "with French serges and with bed-curtains", and finally, carpets being spread upon the grass near the lake, there they tell their tales.
