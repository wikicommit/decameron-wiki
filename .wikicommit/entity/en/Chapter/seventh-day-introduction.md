The *Introduction* to the Seventh Day is the section that opens the seventh day of the [[Book/decameron]] and precedes the First Tale. It records the only movement of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] that is not a change of residence: the day is spent entirely in the [[Place/valle-delle-donne]], discovered by the women the previous afternoon, and the company goes there at dawn to return only in the evening.

## Contents

Every star has already fled from the eastern quarter, save that one "alone which we call Lucifer", when the steward rises and goes into the valley "with a great train of baggage" to dispose everything there according to the order received from his lord. Shortly afterwards the king [[Person/dioneo]] rises, wakened by the din of the loaders and of the beasts, and has the women and the young men rise likewise; all set out before the sun's rays have well appeared.

It had never before seemed to them that the nightingales and the other birds sang so gaily as that morning; accompanied by their songs they reach the valley, where they are received by many more birds, and it seems to them that these rejoice at their coming. Going through it and surveying it anew, it appears to them as much fairer than on the day before "as the hour of the day was more conformable to its beauty".

Their fast being broken with good wine and comfits, and so as not to be outdone in song by the birds, they themselves begin to sing; and the text records the echo of the valley, which answers "always saying those same songs that they said", while the birds, "as though unwilling to be vanquished", add to them "sweet and new notes". The hour of eating being come, the tables are set beneath "the lively laurels and the other fair trees", near the fair little lake; and as they eat they see the fish swimming through the lake "in very great shoals", a thing that affords matter at once for looking and sometimes for discourse.

## Framing and Significance

The viands and the tables being removed, they return to singing "still more gladly than before". Beds are then made in several places of the little valley, all girt and enclosed by the "discreet steward" with "French serges and with bed-curtains", and by the king's leave whoever wishes may go to sleep, while whoever does not may take at pleasure the other accustomed delights. The hour being come to gather for the storytelling, and not far from the place where they had eaten, carpets are spread upon the grass; having seated themselves near the lake, the king commands [[Person/emilia]] to begin, and she begins gladly to speak, smiling.

The section is notable for the way in which the place enters into the storytelling rather than merely housing it: the valley that repeats the company's songs and the fish observed during the meal are elements of the landscape that the text integrates into the scansion of the day, and the making of the beds among the trees transfers into the open air the whole domestic routine of the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]].
