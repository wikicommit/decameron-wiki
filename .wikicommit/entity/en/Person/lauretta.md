Lauretta is one of the seven young women of the [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of the [[Book/decameron]]. Her name is a clear reference to the muse of [[Person/petrarch]], Laura de Noves, whose name comes from the laurel, the plant that is the symbol of glory.

Together with [[Person/neifile]], [[Person/emilia]] and [[Person/fiammetta]], Lauretta belongs to the brigata without playing an active role, and stands out little within the group.

## Role in the brigata

Lauretta is the queen of the Eighth Day and connects herself to the theme chosen by [[Person/dioneo]] on the preceding day, choosing as her theme the tricks that men and women play upon one another.

## Her tales

Recurrent in Lauretta's tales is the theme bound to avarice, to jealousy and to wrath.

## Her reign

Lauretta governs the Eighth Day. At the end of the Seventh, [[Person/dioneo]] places the crown upon her, saying: "My lady, I crown you with your own self queen of our brigata". She at once has the seneschal called and bids him have the tables set in the [[Place/valle-delle-donne]] "at somewhat better an hour than is usual", so that afterwards they may return at leisure to the palace.

She enunciates the theme with a witticism directed at her predecessor: since Dioneo had wished that they discourse of the tricks that women play upon their husbands, were it not that she does not wish to show herself "of the breed of the cur, that will at once be avenged", she would say that tomorrow they should discourse of the tricks that men play upon their wives. Instead she establishes a broader theme: "of those tricks that all day long either woman plays upon man, or man upon woman, or one man upon another" (see [[DefinedTerm/beffa]]). Finally, remembering that the following day is Friday, she renews the abstention from telling tales for that day and for the Saturday, declaring that she wishes to follow "the good example given us by [[Person/neifile]]".

## In the song of the brigata

Lauretta is called upon to sing at the end of the Third Day by the king [[Person/filostrato]]. She excuses herself, saying that she knows no songs of others' making, nor has in mind any of her own "that is very suitable to so merry a company"; but, encouraged by the king, she sings "with a voice sweet enough, but in a manner somewhat piteous" the ballata "Niuna sconsolata". The text notes that the song was remarked by all and "was differently understood by different people": there were those who would understand it "in the Milanese fashion, that a good pig is better than a fair lass", while others were "of a more sublime and better and truer understanding" (see [[DefinedTerm/ballata]]).
