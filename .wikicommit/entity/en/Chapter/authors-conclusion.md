The *Author's Conclusion* is the section with which the [[Book/decameron]] closes after the Tenth Day. In it the author speaks in the first person, addressing directly the "most noble young ladies" for whose consolation he declares he undertook "so long a labour": he considers that he has fully supplied what at the beginning of the work he had promised to do, and judges that the time has come to "give rest to the pen and to the wearied hand".

Before granting it, however, he proposes to answer briefly "certain little matters" that one of his women readers or others might raise — "as though to tacitly posed questions". The section is thus a self-defence built out of imagined objections and replies, and the author himself connects it to a precedent within the work, recalling that he had already shown at the beginning of the Fourth Day that these matters have no special privilege above the rest.

The piece closes with thanks to God, a leave-taking of the women readers, and the explicit formula that seals the book: "here ends the tenth and last day of the book called decameron".

## Contents

**The licence of speech.** To the first objection — that he has used too much licence, at times making women say and more often hear things unbecoming — the author replies by denying the premise: nothing is so dishonourable that, said in honourable words, it ill befits anyone. Then, granting the contrary for the sake of argument, he adduces several reasons. The first is that the qualities of the tales required it: whoever looks at them with a reasonable eye will see that, if one did not wish to draw them out of their proper form, they could not have been told otherwise. The second is that women "with whom words weigh more than deeds" have no right to reproach him for words that men and women say all day long — "hole", "peg", "mortar", "pestle", "sausage", "salami". The third is that no less authority should be granted to his pen than to the painter's brush, which without just reproof makes Saint Michael wound the serpent with a sword or with a lance and Saint George the dragon wherever it pleases him, makes Adam male and Eve female, and nails Christ's feet to the cross now with one nail and now with two.

**The place of the telling.** There follows the argument from circumstance: these things were not said in church, nor in the schools of philosophers, nor among clerics nor among philosophers, but "in gardens, in a place of recreation, among young people, though mature and not to be swayed by tales", and at a time in which "to go about with one's breeches on one's head for the sake of one's own escape" was not unbecoming to the most virtuous. The tales, whatever they may be, can harm and benefit like all other things, according to the hearer: wine is an excellent thing for the living but harmful to one who has a fever; fire is most useful but burns houses and cities; arms defend the safety of those who wish to live peaceably and yet also kill, not through any malice of their own but through that of whoever wickedly wields them. The principle is formulated in a maxim — "no corrupt mind ever understood a word soundly" — and carried to its extreme case: no book is holier than divine Scripture, and yet many, understanding it perversely, have drawn themselves and others to perdition.

**The less fair tales.** To the second objection — that it would have been better if certain tales had not been there — the author concedes the point but denies that it can be charged to him: he could not and should not have written any but those that were told, and it was for those who told them to have told them well. And even if one wished to presuppose that he had been their inventor as well as their writer — which he denies having been — he would not be ashamed of it, because no master save God does everything well and completely: Charlemagne, "first maker of paladins", was not able to create so many of them as to make an army of them alone, and no field was ever so well cultivated that among the better plants there were not found mingled a nettle or a thistle or a briar. To the reader the author further offers a practical instrument: the tales "all bear marked on their brow that which they keep hidden within their bosom", so that one may leave aside those that sting and read those that delight.

**The length.** To the third objection he replies that whoever has other things to do is a fool to read them, even were they short, and he recalls having offered his labour "to the idle and not to the others": to one who reads to pass the time nothing can be long. Brief things suit students, who labour not to pass but to employ their time usefully; and since none of his women readers goes to study "either at Athens or at Bologna or at Paris", one must speak with them more at length than with those whose wits have been sharpened by study.

**The tone and the friars.** To the fourth objection — that what is said is too full of witticisms and idle talk, and ill befits a weighty and grave man — he gives thanks to whoever raises it, since it proceeds from good zeal and is tender of his reputation; then he confesses to being weighty and to having been weighed many times, but declares to those who have not weighed him that he is so light as to float upon the water. He adds that, if the sermons made by friars to rebuke men for their faults are seen today to be for the most part full of witticisms, idle talk and drolleries, those same things are not out of place in his tales, written "to drive melancholy from women"; and that, to anyone who should laugh too much, the lament of Jeremiah, the passion of the Saviour and the plaint of the Magdalene may readily do good. To the last objection — that he has an evil and venomous tongue, because in certain places he writes the truth about friars — he replies with an openly ironic encomium of friars, which reverses its sense in the very act of pronouncing it: they are good people, "they flee discomfort for the love of God, and they grind when the mill is full and do not tell of it", and were it not that they all "smell a little of the goat", their pleading would be far more agreeable. He confesses at last that the things of this world have no stability but are always in flux, and so it may have happened with his tongue: a neighbour of his, not long before, had told him that he had "the best and the sweetest in the world" — and in truth, when this was said, "there were few left to write of the aforesaid tales", that is, few remained to be written.

## Framing and Significance

The section is built entirely as a reply: in it the author addresses the same female audience for which he declares he undertook the labour, and he anticipates their objections instead of awaiting them. The relation to the beginning of the work is declared by the text itself, which says that he has fully supplied what "at the beginning of the present work" had been promised, and refers back to a precedent within it — the beginning of the Fourth Day — where the same question had already been addressed.

In replying, the author presents himself at once as a writer and as a mere transcriber of others' words: the argument "I could not and should not write any but those that were told" presupposes that the tales were told by others and not invented by him, and that it fell to "those women who told them" to have told them well; immediately afterwards, however, he reserves the right to reply on the contrary hypothesis as well, declaring that he would in any case not be ashamed if not all of them were fair.

The criterion of reading enunciated there is at once a declaration of poetics and a leave-taking: responsibility for meaning is shifted onto the hearer and the reader, each thing being "in itself good for something, and ill employed" able to be harmful to many.

His discourse concluded, the author humbly thanks "him who after so long a labour has with his help" brought him to the desired end, and leaves the "pleasant ladies" in peace, asking that they remember him "if perchance the reading of them profits any of them in anything".
