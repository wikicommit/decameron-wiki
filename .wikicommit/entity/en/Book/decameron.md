The *Decameron* — also attested under the title *Decamerone* — is a collection of a hundred tales written by [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] in the fourteenth century, probably between 1349, the year following the [[Event/black-death-1348]] epidemic in Europe, and 1351 according to the thesis of [[Person/vittore-branca]] or 1353 according to that of Giuseppe Billanovich. The encyclopaedic source devoted to the author instead dates its completion to 1351, noting that more modern studies indicate 1355.

The work is considered one of the most important of fourteenth-century European literature and is the progenitor of prose literature in the Italian vernacular. In it Boccaccio depicts the whole society of his time, integrating the aristocratic ideal of life — founded on courtly love, magnanimity and liberality — with the values of commerce: intelligence, enterprise, cunning. In the words of Francesco De Sanctis, father of Italian literary historiography, the *Decameron* is "the earthly Comedy".

The book tells of a [[DefinedTerm/brigata]] of young people who for ten days stay outside [[Place/florence]], moving to a villa in the hills of the Florentine countryside to escape the plague raging in the city, and who tell each other in turn tales often humorous in cast and with frequent echoes of the bucolic eroticism of the time. For this last aspect the book was charged with immorality or scandal and was in many periods censored, or at any rate not adequately considered in the history of literature.

## The Title and the Subtitle

*Decameron* derives from the Greek δέκα (*déka*, "ten") and ἡμερῶν (genitive plural of ἡμέρα, *hēméra*, "day"), literally "of ten days". The title refers back to the *Exameron* ("of six days") of Saint Ambrose, a reformulation in verse of the biblical account of Genesis: Boccaccio's intention is to construct an analogy between his own work and that of the saint, since as Ambrose narrates the creation of the world and of humanity, so the *Decameron* narrates its *re*-creation by means of the ten protagonists and their storytelling, after the scourge of the plague that fell upon Florence in 1348. The Greek title is also a symptom of the enthusiastic rediscovery of the classics of Hellenic comedy and tragedy.

The heading of the work in Francesco Mannelli's apograph, the [[Manuscript/mannelli-codex]] dated 13 August 1384, reads: "Here begins the book called Decameron, surnamed prince Galeotto. In which are contained a hundred tales told in ten days by seven ladies and by three young men." By "surnamed Prencipe Galeotto" reference is made to Galeuth or Galehaut, a character of the Breton cycle of courtly romance who acted as a go-between in love for Lancelot and Guinevere; "Galeotto" further echoes the line of the fifth canto of [[Person/dante-alighieri]]'s *Inferno*, *Galeotto fu 'l libro e chi lo scrisse*, with which Francesca ends her account.

The rubric that opens the [[Chapter/proem]] in the philological edition on Wikisource, based on the printed edition indexed as *Boccaccio - Decameron I.djvu*, gives only the formula "here begins the book called decameron, in which are contained a hundred tales told in ten days by seven ladies and by three young men", without the surname *prencipe Galeotto* attested in the Mannelli apograph; the source gives the rubric entirely in lower case.

It should be noted that the time actually spent outside the city by the young people is fourteen days, since Friday is devoted to prayer and Saturday to the ladies' personal grooming.

## The Proem

The [[Chapter/proem]] sets out the motives for the writing of the work. Boccaccio states that the book is dedicated to those afflicted by the pains of love, with the aim of delighting them with pleasant stories and of giving them useful counsel, and specifies that the work is addressed in particular to an audience of women, and more precisely to *those who love*. Its intended recipient is the urban bourgeoisie, set in contrast to the institution of the court that had developed above all in France: the tale, characterised by a simple, brief and immediate style, tends to engage with this new social class — secular, well-off and educated — of which Boccaccio is an expression.

Also in the proem Boccaccio declares that he addresses women in order to remedy the *sin of Fortune*: women, owing to the customs of the time, were barred from the diversions granted to men, such as hunting, gaming or trading, so that they could find few distractions from the pains of love. The text of the Proem justifies this choice with an autobiographical argument: Boccaccio declares that from his earliest youth he was kindled by a love "most lofty and noble" and that he owes to the "pleasant discourse" and consolations of a friend the relief he obtained from it, so that out of gratitude — a virtue he calls "supremely to be commended" — he now intends in his turn to offer "some alleviation" to those who have greater need of it. It is in the Proem, moreover, that the author announces his narrative programme: to relate "a hundred tales, or fables or parables or histories, whichever we choose to call them", told in ten days by an "honourable company of seven ladies and three young men" in the "pestilential season of the late mortality", together with certain little songs sung by the ladies themselves; from their reading the addressees will be able to draw both delight and "useful counsel", recognising "what is to be shunned and what is likewise to be followed". From the proem onward the theme of love shows its importance, taking on licentious forms as well; for this reason Boccaccio, in the introduction to the Fourth Day — where the so-called "apologue of the goslings" is placed — and in the conclusion of the work, claims his own right to a literature that is free and inspired by a naturalistic conception of Eros.

## Plot and Structure

The names of the ten young protagonists are Fiammetta, Filomena, Emilia, Elissa, Lauretta, Neifile, Pampinea, Dioneo, Filostrato and Panfilo; all are of elevated social condition. Each day has a *king* or a *queen* who sets the theme of the tales, by which all must be guided; the first and the ninth days, however, have a free theme for everyone. To Dioneo alone, on account of his youth, it is granted not to observe the established theme, on condition that he always tells his tale last: this is the so-called "Dioneo's privilege".

Among the principal themes are love and fortune, upon which others are grafted that become the specific subject of the individual days: apart from the First and the Ninth, whose subject is free, the ten storytellers are called upon to discourse on cases of fortune with a happy ending (II), on desires fulfilled through one's own "industry" (III), on happy loves (V), on witticisms capable of saving one who is in danger of death or of extricating one who finds himself in difficult situations (VI), on the tricks played by wives upon their husbands (VII) or on those that women and men play upon one another (VIII), and finally on liberal and magnificent deeds (X). The Fourth Day is devoted to loves *that came to an unhappy end*.

As written in the conclusion of the work, the themes are essentially two: to show the Florentines that, with the right spirit, it is possible to rise again from any misfortune, just as the ten young people do in the face of the plague; and Boccaccio's respect for women, to whom the work is dedicated because at that time they were the people who read most and had the most time to devote to reading.

Regarding the overall structure numerous interpretations have been formulated. [[Person/vittore-branca]] posited an ascending structure, in which the negative example of [[ShortStory/ser-ciappelletto]], protagonist of the first tale of the first day, is set against the positive one of Griselda, character of the last tale of the work, and in general against the protagonists of the whole tenth day, whose assigned themes are liberality and magnanimity. Alberto Asor Rosa has posited a structuring by "thematic clusters", formed of several days characterised by similar themes; the critic Ferdinando Neri instead attempted to divide the work into two parts of five days each, in which the first and the sixth days serve as introductions.

## Themes, Sources and Language

The conception of moral life in the *Decameron* is based on the contrast between [[DefinedTerm/fortune-and-nature]], *the two ministers of the world* (VI, 2, 6). Human wit is another recurring motif: here are found the taste for the trick of Chichibio (VI, 4), the impious unscrupulousness of Ciappelletto (I, 1), the gullibility of Andreuccio da Perugia (II, 5) and of Calandrino, the wit and swindling of Frate Cipolla (VI, 10), the courteous wit of Cisti the baker (VI, 2), the ready intelligence of Melchisedech (I, 3), the ingenuity of Giotto (VI, 5) and the lordliness shot through with eccentricity of the brigand Ghino di Tacco (X, 2). Two days are consecrated to witticisms, four to cunning of every kind.

The work presents a twofold "soul": the first realistic, reflecting the mentality and culture of the bourgeois-mercantile class — Vittore Branca has defined Boccaccio's work as a "mercantile epic" — the second aristocratic, in which the chivalric virtues of the feudal aristocracy and of the courtly world are present: courtesy, magnanimity, munificence, loyalty, human virtue even unto sacrifice, as in the tales of the tenth day and in [[ShortStory/federigo-degli-alberighi]] (V, 9). Various aspects of the *Decameron* anticipate fifteenth-century Humanism: the interest in man and social life, the exaltation of intelligence, love considered as a natural feeling, nature represented as a place of peace and serenity.

The structure sinks its roots into distant traditions: the recourse to the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] was typical of the Eastern and Arabic novella tradition; the idea of a company of ten people conversing after dinner over several days is already in the *Saturnalia* of Macrobius; the strand of the *Menippean Satires* influences the *Metamorphoses* of Apuleius, in which the theme of telling tales in a situation of danger, in the face of death, also appears. As a thematic repertory Boccaccio drew on numerous medieval sources: the *fabliaux*, the *lais*, the cantari of the jongleurs, the collections of *exempla*, the *vidas* of the troubadours, the elegiac comedies in Latin, Eastern fables, and at times the very material of the *Novellino*.

The language adapts itself to the variety of settings, characters and places: the period is at times ample and solemn, rich in subordinate clauses, parentheses, inversions and Latinate constructions, at other times swifter; the lexicon ranges from a lofty and elegant choice to a picturesque and slangy manner of speaking. There are moreover three levels of narration: Boccaccio the author is the omniscient narrator of the first level, the narrators of the tales are of the second level, and the protagonists who in their turn tell a story — such as Melchisedech — are of the third level. Story and plot generally coincide, but there is no lack of analepses, of accelerations obtained through summaries or ellipses, and of retardations resulting from digressions or descriptive pauses.

## Transmission and Reception

In 1370 Boccaccio transcribed in his own hand a codex of the work, the [[Manuscript/hamilton-90]]; the apograph of Francesco Mannelli, dated 1384, is another early witness to it. The *editio princeps*, the so-called *Deo gratias*, dates perhaps to 1470, and an illustrated edition was printed in Venice by Giovanni and Gregorio de Gregorii on 20 June 1492. Among modern critical editions the one prepared by Vittore Branca according to the Hamilton autograph, published by the Accademia della Crusca in 1976, stands out.

The *Decameron* enjoyed enormous success from the time of the author's death onward: [[Person/pietro-bembo]] made of it in 1525, with the *Prose della volgar lingua*, the model of Italian prose, while earlier Lorenzo de' Medici had praised it as a great work. It nevertheless suffered a clear condemnation from the Council of Trent, at the origin of the long episode of the [[DefinedTerm/decameron-censorship]]. Only with the beginning of the contemporary age did the *Decameron* begin to be reconsidered by critics.

The spread of the work, according to what Vittore Branca had occasion to say, "is more European than Italian": its circulation in France, Spain, Germany and above all England was unprecedented. In the Anglo-Saxon world Boccaccio was the inspiration for [[Person/geoffrey-chaucer]], author of *The Canterbury Tales*, a work with a wholly similar structure and narrative frame, although Chaucer gathered no more than 24 tales instead of the hoped-for 100. In the eighteenth century the work also appeared in Russia with its first translation; Nikolay Chernyshevsky has the protagonist of the novel *What Is to Be Done?* say that Boccaccio's tales express "fresh, pure, luminous thoughts" and can be set "beside the best Shakespearean dramas for the depth and subtlety of their psychological analysis". The last tale, that of Griselda, was translated into Latin by [[Person/petrarch]].
