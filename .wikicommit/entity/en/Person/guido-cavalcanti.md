Guido, son of messer Cavalcante de' Cavalcanti, is the Florentine gentleman protagonist of the ninth tale of the Sixth Day of the [[Book/decameron]] (see [[ShortStory/guido-cavalcanti-tombs]]). The text presents him as "one of the best logicians the world had, and an excellent natural philosopher", and at the same time as a man "most graceful and well-mannered and a great talker", able to do better than anyone else "everything that he wished to do and that pertained to a gentleman".

To his learning the tale joins his social condition: he was "most rich", and "as to asking with the tongue, he knew how to honour whomever it came into his mind was worth it". Precisely for the whole of these qualities messer Betto Brunelleschi and his companions had greatly striven to draw him into their company — although they cared little, the text specifies, for his gifts as logician and philosopher.

## Background

The tale places him at Florence, where he often made his way from Orto San Michele along the Corso degli Adimari as far as San Giovanni. No attempt of messer Betto's to have him in his own company ever succeeded; and messer Betto and his companions believed that this depended on the fact that Guido, "speculating, became much abstracted from men".

The text reports besides that he "held somewhat of the opinion of the Epicureans", and that for this reason "it was said among the common people" that his speculations were only a searching whether it might be found that God did not exist. The story presents this as a rumour current among the vulgar, not as a position declared by him; and it is upon this that the knights build the provocation from which the episode arises.

In the scene narrated, surprised among the marble tombs near San Giovanni by the company that comes up on horseback, and seeing himself shut in by them, Guido answers: "Gentlemen, you may say to me in your own house whatever you please"; then, laying his hand upon one of the great tombs, "like one who was exceedingly nimble", he vaults over it and goes away. The sense of the phrase will be made clear only afterwards, by messer Betto, as the greatest incivility said seemly and in few words. From then on the knights never troubled him again.

## Works & Achievements

The text names no work of Guido's nor reports any writing of his: it records his intellectual qualities — logic and natural philosophy — and the fame that derived from them, together with the tendency to abstraction that separated him from men while he speculated.

The only act of his that the tale reports at length is the retort addressed to the company, which the narrator announces in her exordium as "such a retort that perhaps there has been none of so much meaning recounted here".
