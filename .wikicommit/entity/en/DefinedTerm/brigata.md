The *brigata* is the group of ten young protagonists of the [[Book/decameron]]: seven young women and three young men, all of elevated social condition, who during the [[Event/black-death-1348]] meet in the church of [[Place/santa-maria-novella]] and decide to seek escape from the contagion by moving to the countryside, on the hills near [[Place/florence]]. Boccaccio calls it the "honourable brigata".

The expression is attested as early as the [[Chapter/proem]] of the work, where Boccaccio announces his intention to relate a hundred tales "told in ten days by an honourable company of seven ladies and three young men in the pestilential season of the late mortality", together with certain little songs sung by the ladies themselves: the composition of the group, the duration of ten days and the circumstance of the pestilence are therefore declared by the author even before the frame narrative is staged. The rubric that opens the book records the ten narrators in the same proportion, announcing "a hundred tales told in ten days by seven ladies and three young men".

## The Formation of the Group

The actual birth of the brigata is narrated in the [[Chapter/first-day-introduction]]. On a Tuesday morning, while the city is "almost empty of inhabitants", seven young women in mourning dress find themselves by chance in Santa Maria Novella after hearing the divine offices: they are all bound to one another by friendship, neighbourhood or kinship, none has passed twenty-eight years nor is younger than eighteen, and they are "each of them discreet and of noble blood and fair of form and adorned with good manners and with graceful virtue". Boccaccio declares that he does not wish to report their real names, so as not to expose them in future to shame and not to give matter to the envious, and that he will instead name them "by names suited to the qualities of each, either wholly or in part": [[Person/pampinea]], the eldest, then [[Person/fiammetta]], [[Person/filomena]], [[Person/emilia]], [[Person/lauretta]], [[Person/neifile]] and [[Person/elissa]].

It is Pampinea who proposes that they leave the city and go to stay in the country places that each of them has at her disposal. Filomena objects that women, left to themselves, hardly know how to govern themselves "without the providence of some man", and Elissa adds that their menfolk are for the most part dead and the others scattered among various companies. While they are debating, three young men enter the church — [[Person/panfilo]], [[Person/filostrato]] and [[Person/dioneo]] — none of them younger than twenty-five; all three are in love with one of the seven ladies present. Pampinea, joined to one of them by consanguinity, entreats them on behalf of them all to keep them company "with a pure and brotherly spirit"; the young men, having at first believed themselves mocked, gladly accept.

The departure takes place the following morning, the Wednesday, at daybreak: the ladies with some of their maidservants and the three young men with three of their retainers leave the city and, without going more than two short miles from it, reach the appointed place — a palace on the summit of a little hill, with a fair and spacious courtyard, loggias, halls and chambers adorned with cheerful paintings, meadows, gardens, wells of the freshest water and vaults of precious wines.

## The Regulations of the First Day

Once the company has reached the palace, Pampinea observes that "things that are without order cannot long endure" and proposes that there be among them a chief, honoured and obeyed as their superior, and that the burden and the honour of the lordship fall to each for one day, so that no one may envy it; the first will be elected by all, the following ones designated as vespers draws near by whoever has ruled that day. Elected queen of the first day with one voice, Pampinea receives from Filomena a laurel garland which, set upon her head, will remain for the whole duration of the company "a manifest sign to every other of the royal lordship and pre-eminence".

As queen, Pampinea distributes the offices among the seven servants in attendance — the three retainers of the young men and the four maidservants: Parmeno, Dioneo's retainer, is made seneschal, with charge of the whole household and of the service of the hall; Sirisco, Panfilo's retainer, steward and treasurer, bound to follow Parmeno's commands; Tindaro attends to the chambers of the three young men; Misia, Pampinea's maid, and Licisca, Filomena's, are assigned to the kitchen; Chimera, Lauretta's, and Stratilia, Fiammetta's, to the keeping of the ladies' chambers and the cleanliness of the place. The queen further commands them all that, wherever they may go and whatever they may hear or see, "no tidings other than glad ones" be brought in from outside.

The day is then ordered by the queen: a walk in the garden until the hour of terce, then dinner in a ground-floor hall with the whitest tablecloths, glasses that seemed of silver and everything strewn with broom flowers; after the tables are cleared, carols and songs, with Dioneo at the lute and Fiammetta at the viol; then rest, and finally the gathering in a little shaded meadow, where Pampinea proposes to pass the hot part of the day not in playing at backgammon and chess — games in which the mind of one of the parties must needs be troubled — but in telling tales, which affords delight to the whole company that listens. For the first day she sets a free theme, and gives a beginning to the storytelling by turning to Panfilo, seated on her right.

## Composition and Operation

The names of the ten young people are [[Person/fiammetta]], [[Person/filomena]], [[Person/emilia]], [[Person/elissa]], [[Person/lauretta]], [[Person/neifile]], [[Person/pampinea]], [[Person/dioneo]], [[Person/filostrato]] and [[Person/panfilo]]. For two weeks the brigata entertains itself serenely with various pastimes — songs, dances and games, besides prayers — and in particular by telling tales in turn.

To occupy the early afternoon hours the young people decide to tell one tale each daily, except on Friday and Saturday, following a precise set of rules: each day, in turn, a *king* or a *queen* is elected who fixes the theme of the day, by which all must be guided. The first and the ninth days have a free theme for everyone. To Dioneo alone, on account of his youth, it is granted not to observe the established theme, provided that he always tells his tale last: this is the so-called "Dioneo's privilege".

The singular names attributed to the narrators have received many interpretations, largely echoing Greek etymologies: Dioneo ("lustful", from Dione, mother of Venus), Filostrato ("vanquished by love"), Panfilo (the "All Love"), Elissa (the other name of Dido, queen of the *Aeneid*), Emilia ("she who is proud of her beauty"), Fiammetta (the maiden who burns with love like a flame), Filomena ("lover of song" or else "she who is loved"), Lauretta (like Laura de Noves, whose name comes from the laurel), Neifile ("new lover") and Pampinea ("the luxuriant one").

Within the group Pampinea, Filomena and Elissa stand out for their sense of initiative, while Neifile, Emilia, Fiammetta and Lauretta, though part of the brigata, play no active role, do not intervene in the decisions and may be considered background figures.

The life of the brigata constitutes one of the narrative levels into which the [[DefinedTerm/frame-narrative]] of the work is articulated, alongside the plane of the author, that of the tales and that of the reflections that the storytellers elaborate as a preamble to, or in the wake of, the individual tales. According to Maurizio Fiorilla, "the formation of the brigata described in the second part of the *Introduction* to the First Day creates the conditions for a reconstruction of the world through literature".

## The Dissolution of the Company

The overall duration of the retreat is stated by the king [[Person/panfilo]] in the Conclusion of the Tenth Day: "tomorrow it will be a fortnight", he says, since they left [[Place/florence]] to take some recreation for the sustenance of their health and their life. The fifteen days therefore comprise, besides the ten days of storytelling, the days of suspension observed for Friday and Saturday and the days of travel.

Panfilo also draws up the reckoning of their life together: although glad tales have been told "and perhaps conducive to concupiscence", and although the company has continually eaten and drunk well, played and sung — "all things apt to incite weak minds to less than honourable things" — he declares that he has known among them "no act, no word, nothing […] to be blamed", but rather "continual virtue, continual concord, continual brotherly familiarity".

Among the arguments he adduces for their return is also the risk that the company may grow: it is "already known round about by several others" and might multiply "in such a fashion" as to take from them all consolation. The decision being taken "as useful and as honourable", the brigata returns to Florence the following morning, and the three young men leave the seven ladies at [[Place/santa-maria-novella]], "whence they had set out with them"; the ladies, when it seems to them time, return to their own homes. The group thus dissolves in the very place where it had been formed.
