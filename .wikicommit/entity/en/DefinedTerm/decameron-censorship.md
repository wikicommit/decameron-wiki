By *censorship of the Decameron* is meant the sixteenth-century episode that led the work of [[Person/giovanni-boccaccio]] to be prohibited and then reprinted in "emended" versions. From the middle of the sixteenth century the system for controlling writings became organised and institutionalised in order to meet the struggle against heresy: thus was established the Index of Prohibited Books, willed by Pope Paul IV Carafa in 1559 as a "filter" to counter the accusations, even veiled ones, of the writers of the time. The order from Rome was peremptory: "...In no way let there be talk in ill or scandal of priests, friars, abbots, abbesses, monks, nuns, parish priests, provosts, bishops, or other sacred things, but let the names be changed; or let it be done in whatever other way shall seem better".

The [[Book/decameron]] appeared in the Index under the letter B with the Latin formula "Boccacci Decades seu novellae centum quae hactenus cum intolerabilibus erroribus impressae sunt et quae posterum cum eisdem erroribus imprimentur", that is, "Boccaccio's Decades or Hundred Tales, which up to now have been printed with intolerable errors and which in future will be printed with the same errors".

## The Emendation by the Deputies (1573)

In 1573 the Inquisition commissioned Florentine experts, the *Deputies*, with the task of "setting in order" the Florentine text par excellence. There is no agreement on the identity of the Deputies charged with the revision: a first hypothesis counts three of them — [[Person/vincenzo-borghini]], Pierfrancesco Cambi and Sebastiano Antinori — a second four — [[Person/vincenzo-borghini]], Sebastiano Antinori, Agnolo Guicciardini and Antonio Benivieni. Among the members of the group Borghini stands out, recognised as the true promoter of the work's censorship.

Having received from the Church of Rome the *Decameron* marked in the passages to be modified, the Deputies proceeded with various weapons — cultural, traditional, philological and rhetorical arguments — to the defence of the text, attempting to save what could be saved. The censorship proper thus fell directly to the Church of Rome, while the linguistic and philological expertise fell to the Deputies. On 2 May 1572 the official copy authorised by the Inquisitor of Rome for printing returned to Florence, but only on 17 August 1573 was the text printed; the following year the abridged work was accompanied by *Le Annotazioni di discorsi sopra alcuni luoghi del Decameron*, a collection of linguistic and philological considerations intended to justify the choices made during the individual phases of the emendation. The Deputies' *Decameron* found itself prohibited shortly afterwards by the Inquisition itself, and consequently went through only one edition.

## Salviati's Emendation (1582)

In 1582 the *Decameron* had another edition, prepared by [[Person/leonardo-salviati]]. It seems that it was Salviati himself who urged the Roman curia, through his protector Jacopo Buoncompagni, to call for a new censorship of the work. The hypothesis that the new emendation became necessary because the Deputies had shown a certain negligence in the sphere of morals, especially sexual morals, is unfounded.

In reality Salviati's *Decameron* appears, rather than a true edition founded on original research, a correction of the preceding edition. Whereas Borghini's Deputies limited themselves to cutting, Salviati modified: that is, he intervened not only upon the text but also upon its reading, resorting to marginal glosses in order openly to perform a mediating function between text and reader and to impose a univocal interpretation. Salviati's operation spared 48 tales and modified 52.
